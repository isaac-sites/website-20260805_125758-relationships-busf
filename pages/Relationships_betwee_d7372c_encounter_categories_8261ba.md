## Introduction

The categories do not classify UFOs as extraterrestrial craft; they classify the structure of an encounter report. A “close encounter of the third kind”, for example, means that a witness reports seeing occupants or entities associated with a UFO, not that alien life has been scientifically confirmed. This distinction helped the terminology move from UFO research into films, novels and television, where encounter types became narrative tools for exploring fear, curiosity, communication and the possibility of contact with other intelligences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.howstuffworks.com/space/aliens-ufos/ufo-classification.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.howstuffworks.com">[HowStuffWorks Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.howstuffworks.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">How Stuff Works Science UFO Classification &#124; How Stuff Works</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">How Stuff Works Science UFO Classification &#124; How Stuff Works</span></span></span>

## How Hynek’s encounter scale changed UFO language

### From sightings to interaction

J. Allen Hynek developed his close encounter categories while working as a scientific consultant on United States Air Force UFO investigations and later refined them in his 1972 book *The UFO Experience: A Scientific Inquiry*. His aim was not to prove a particular explanation for [UFO reports]({{ 'ufo-reports/' | relative_url }}) but to create a more consistent way of describing unusual observations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.exopaedia.org/Hynek%2C%2BJoseph%2BAllen" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: exopaedia.org">[Exopaedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">exopaedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Hynek, Joseph Allen</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Exopaedia:: Hynek, Joseph Allen...</span></span></span>

The original system began with three close encounter categories:

* **Close Encounter of the [First Kind]({{ 'first-kind/' | relative_url }}) (CE1):** a nearby visual sighting of an unidentified object without reported physical effects.
* **Close Encounter of the [Second Kind]({{ 'second-kind/' | relative_url }}) (CE2):** a sighting accompanied by claimed physical effects, such as interference with equipment, effects on the environment or traces left at a location.
* **Close Encounter of the Third Kind (CE3):** a report involving visible entities or occupants connected with the object.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.howstuffworks.com/space/aliens-ufos/ufo-classification.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.howstuffworks.com">[HowStuffWorks Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.howstuffworks.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">How Stuff Works Science UFO Classification &#124; How Stuff Works</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">How Stuff Works Science UFO Classification &#124; How Stuff Works</span></span></span>

The significance of the scale was cultural as much as investigative. Before the phrase became popular, UFO reports were often grouped simply as sightings. Hynek’s categories introduced a way of discussing degrees of reported involvement, allowing researchers, journalists and fictional creators to distinguish between observing a mysterious object and experiencing a claimed encounter with a living presence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cufos.org/types-of-ufos/classification-systems/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufos.org">[Center for UFO Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Center for UFO Studies Classification Systems</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Center for UFO StudiesClassification Systems - Center for UFO Studies...</span></span></span>

## What each encounter category means

### First kind: seeing an unknown object

A first-kind encounter is the most basic form of close encounter: the witness reports seeing an unusual object at relatively close range but does not report measurable effects. These accounts often involve unusual lights, shapes or movements that the observer cannot immediately identify.

Within [UFO culture]({{ 'ufo-culture/' | relative_url }}) and science fiction, this category represents the moment of uncertainty before contact. Many stories begin with a distant observation that challenges existing assumptions: a pilot tracking something unknown, a person seeing a strange light over a landscape, or scientists detecting an unexplained object. The dramatic importance comes from ambiguity rather than direct interaction.

### Second kind: when the encounter leaves traces

Second-kind encounters add a physical dimension. Witnesses may report effects such as marks on the ground, changes to vegetation, interference with vehicles or electronics, or unusual reactions from animals. These cases attracted particular attention because they appeared to offer something that could be examined beyond personal testimony.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.howstuffworks.com/space/aliens-ufos/ufo-classification.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.howstuffworks.com">[HowStuffWorks Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.howstuffworks.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">How Stuff Works Science UFO Classification &#124; How Stuff Works</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">How Stuff Works Science UFO Classification &#124; How Stuff Works</span></span></span>

A famous example often discussed in UFO literature is the 1951 Lubbock Lights case, where witnesses reported groups of glowing objects over Texas. Although the event generated extensive investigation and debate, it did not produce evidence demonstrating an extraterrestrial cause. Such cases illustrate the central tension of UFO research: a report can be unusual and historically influential while still lacking proof of its interpretation.

For science fiction, second-kind encounters provide a bridge between observation and material evidence. Stories use physical traces as a way to suggest that an unknown visitor has affected the human world, creating questions about technology, biology and the limits of human understanding.

### Third kind: reported occupants and the idea of contact

Third-kind encounters involve reports of beings, occupants or entities associated with a UFO. This category became internationally recognised partly through Steven Spielberg’s 1977 film *Close Encounters of the Third Kind*, which helped transform Hynek’s terminology into a mainstream cultural phrase.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.paranormal-encyclopedia.com/u/ufo/close-encounters/first-kind.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: paranormal-encyclopedia.com">[Paranormal Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">paranormal-encyclopedia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Paranormal Encyclopedia Close Encounters of the First Kind</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Paranormal Encyclopedia Close Encounters of the First Kind</span></span></span>

The category is particularly important in science fiction because it moves the story from discovery to relationship. An unidentified object alone raises questions about technology and origin; an occupant introduces questions about language, motives, intelligence and ethics.

Many famous fictional alien-contact narratives follow this structure. They use the encounter with a being to explore whether humanity responds with fear, aggression, cooperation or curiosity. In this sense, the third kind became one of the most influential templates for modern [first-contact]({{ 'first-contact/' | relative_url }}) storytelling.

## Later expansions: abductions and communication categories

The original Hynek system stopped at third kind, but later researchers and UFO writers added new categories to describe reports that did not fit easily into the original framework.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cufos.org/types-of-ufos/classification-systems/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufos.org">[Center for UFO Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Center for UFO Studies Classification Systems</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Center for UFO StudiesClassification Systems - Center for UFO Studies...</span></span></span>

### Fourth kind: alleged abduction experiences

The fourth kind usually refers to reports in which a person claims to have been taken aboard a UFO or removed from their normal environment. These accounts became prominent during the late twentieth century, especially through widely discussed abduction narratives.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cufos.org/types-of-ufos/classification-systems/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufos.org">[Center for UFO Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Center for UFO Studies Classification Systems</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Center for UFO StudiesClassification Systems - Center for UFO Studies...</span></span></span>

Abduction stories had a major influence on science fiction because they shifted the focus from public sightings to personal experiences. Instead of a spacecraft appearing over a city, the encounter became intimate and psychological, raising themes of memory, identity, control and the boundary between human experience and external reality.

Researchers and critics have debated these accounts extensively. Supporters argue that some reports contain recurring patterns, while sceptics point to possible explanations involving memory, sleep phenomena, cultural influence and expectations shaped by popular media. The category remains influential primarily as a description of reported experiences rather than as established evidence of alien activity.

### Fifth kind: communication and human-initiated contact

The meaning of “[fifth kind]({{ 'fifth-kind/' | relative_url }})” is less consistent than the earlier categories. Some versions describe direct communication between humans and alleged non-human intelligences, while others associate the term with human-initiated attempts to establish contact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.howstuffworks.com/space/aliens-ufos/ufo-classification.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.howstuffworks.com">[HowStuffWorks Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.howstuffworks.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">How Stuff Works Science UFO Classification &#124; How Stuff Works</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">How Stuff Works Science UFO Classification &#124; How Stuff Works</span></span></span>

This category reflects a broader change in both UFO culture and science fiction. Early stories often imagined invasion or accidental discovery, whereas later works increasingly focus on communication: how different forms of intelligence might understand each other and whether contact would be beneficial or dangerous.

## Why encounter categories matter in science fiction


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

UFO encounter categories influenced science fiction by turning different stages of contact into storytelling structures. A first-kind event creates mystery, a second-kind event introduces evidence, a third-kind event creates dialogue, and later categories explore deeper questions about identity and transformation.

</div>

The terminology also shaped public expectations. The phrase “close encounter” became associated not only with UFO reports but with a wider cultural idea of meeting something unknown. Science fiction adopted these categories because they provided a ready-made framework for stories about discovery and contact, even when writers changed or challenged the original meanings.

At the same time, the categories reveal the difference between classification and explanation. A system can organise reports by what people describe without resolving whether those experiences result from extraterrestrial visitors, misidentified objects, psychological processes, technological events or other causes. Modern discussions of unidentified aerial phenomena continue this distinction by separating the description of an observation from claims about its origin.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.howstuffworks.com/space/aliens-ufos/ufo-classification.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.howstuffworks.com">[HowStuffWorks Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.howstuffworks.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">How Stuff Works Science UFO Classification &#124; How Stuff Works</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">How Stuff Works Science UFO Classification &#124; How Stuff Works</span></span></span>

## The lasting meaning of the encounter scale

UFO encounter categories remain influential because they describe a progression that mirrors one of science fiction’s oldest questions: what happens when humanity meets something genuinely unknown? The movement from sighting to evidence to communication reflects changing ideas about discovery, technology and humanity’s place in the universe.

Although the categories do not provide proof of extraterrestrial contact, they remain valuable cultural tools. They show how UFO reports and science fiction developed a shared language for imagining encounters beyond ordinary human experience, from the first glimpse of a strange object in the sky to the possibility of communication with another form of intelligence.
