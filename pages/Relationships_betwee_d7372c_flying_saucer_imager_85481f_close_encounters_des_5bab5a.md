## Introduction

*Close Encounters of the Third Kind* (1977) changed [the flying saucer]({{ 'the-flying-saucer/' | relative_url }}) from a symbol of invasion into an image of cosmic possibility. Its alien mothership was not designed as a weapon or a machine of domination, but as a vast, mysterious object that could represent communication between civilisations. The final approach of the craft over Devil’s Tower remains one of [science fiction]({{ 'science-fiction/' | relative_url }}) cinema’s defining moments because the ship’s design combines the familiar and the impossible: a structure that resembles a city, an industrial landscape and a luminous piece of alien architecture all at once.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://airandspace.si.edu/air-and-space-quarterly/issue-18/hollywood-close-encounters-model" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airandspace.si.edu">[Smithsonian Air and Space Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airandspace.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Smithsonian Air and Space MuseumThe Mothership’s Stowaways &#124; National Air and Space MuseumMarch 10, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 10, 2026</span></span></span>

The mothership’s importance lies less in technical realism than in the emotional response it creates. Earlier flying saucers often arrived as threats, bringing invasion narratives and Cold War anxieties. Spielberg’s film instead presents the arrival of the craft as a moment of awe, curiosity and potential understanding. The design became a turning point in [UFO imagery]({{ 'ufo-imagery/' | relative_url }}), showing that an alien ship could inspire wonder before fear.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tcm.com/articles/18629/close-encounters-of-the-third-kind-close-encounters-of-the-third-kind" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tcm.com">[Turner Classic Movies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tcm.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Turner Classic Movies Close Encounters Of The Third Kind</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Turner Classic MoviesClose Encounters Of The Third Kind - Close Encounters Of The Third KindDecember 17, 2002...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 17, 2002</span></span></span>

## From invasion craft to cosmic wonder

Before *Close Encounters*, popular science fiction had frequently used flying saucers as symbols of danger. The smooth disc-shaped spacecraft of 1950s cinema could represent an unknown enemy entering human territory. Spielberg retained the mystery of the classic UFO shape but changed its meaning: the [alien craft]({{ 'alien-craft/' | relative_url }}) was no longer primarily something that attacked Earth, but something that invited humanity to make [first contact]({{ 'first-contact/' | relative_url }}).

The film’s narrative builds towards a meeting rather than a battle. The mothership arrives at a secret landing site not to destroy human civilisation but to exchange information, using music and light as a shared language. This approach reflected a broader shift in science fiction during the 1970s, when filmmakers increasingly explored alien encounters as questions about communication, identity and humanity’s place in the universe.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.criterion.com/current/posts/877-close-encounters-of-the-third-kind" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: criterion.com">[The Criterion Collection]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">criterion.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on criterion.com.</span></span></span>

The visual design supports that change in tone. The craft is enormous, but it is not portrayed as a military vessel. It has no visible weapons, aggressive angles or threatening silhouette. Instead, it glows, moves slowly and reveals layers of unexpected detail. Its arrival feels closer to witnessing a natural wonder than an invasion.

This distinction helped redefine the cultural meaning of the flying saucer. The same basic idea — a mysterious object from the sky — could now represent discovery rather than danger. The mothership became an image of humanity standing at the edge of a larger universe.

## Design choices behind the mothership

The mothership was created through a combination of imaginative design, practical model-making and carefully controlled visual effects. The miniature was designed by concept artist Ralph McQuarrie and built by model maker Greg Jein’s team, with visual effects supervised by Douglas Trumbull. Unlike many earlier science-fiction spacecraft that relied on simple rocket shapes or smooth metallic discs, the design aimed to suggest a complex alien environment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://airandspace.si.edu/air-and-space-quarterly/issue-18/hollywood-close-encounters-model" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airandspace.si.edu">[Smithsonian Air and Space Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airandspace.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Smithsonian Air and Space MuseumThe Mothership’s Stowaways &#124; National Air and Space MuseumMarch 10, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 10, 2026</span></span></span>

### A floating city rather than a simple spaceship

One of the most distinctive choices was making the craft feel inhabited. The mothership resembles a vast floating city filled with structures, lights and mechanical layers. The Smithsonian’s National Air and Space Museum describes the model as being designed to convey the feeling of a “floating city”, with details intended to suggest purpose and complexity rather than a generic flying object.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://airandspace.si.edu/air-and-space-quarterly/issue-18/hollywood-close-encounters-model" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airandspace.si.edu">[Smithsonian Air and Space Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airandspace.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Smithsonian Air and Space MuseumThe Mothership’s Stowaways &#124; National Air and Space MuseumMarch 10, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 10, 2026</span></span></span>

This approach changed how audiences imagined [alien technology]({{ 'alien-technology/' | relative_url }}). Instead of asking only “how does this vehicle fly?”, the design encourages viewers to imagine an entire civilisation behind it. The ship appears to contain streets, towers, machinery and hidden spaces, suggesting that the visitors are not merely arriving from another planet but bringing an entire world with them.

The model makers achieved this density through practical techniques such as kit bashing, the process of combining parts from existing model kits and everyday objects to create new forms. Small components from unrelated sources were transformed into convincing alien structures, producing a craft that felt both unfamiliar and physically real.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://airandspace.si.edu/air-and-space-quarterly/issue-18/hollywood-close-encounters-model" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airandspace.si.edu">[Smithsonian Air and Space Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airandspace.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Smithsonian Air and Space MuseumThe Mothership’s Stowaways &#124; National Air and Space MuseumMarch 10, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 10, 2026</span></span></span>

### Light as the language of the unknown

The mothership’s identity comes as much from illumination as from its physical shape. Spielberg and Trumbull avoided making the UFOs look like ordinary machines with visible engineering. Instead, the ships appear almost alive through glowing colours and controlled movement.

Trumbull’s visual effects team used advanced motion-control techniques to combine miniature photography, lighting effects and camera movement with precision. These methods allowed the film to create smooth, dreamlike images of UFOs moving in ways that felt beyond human technology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.criterion.com/current/posts/877-close-encounters-of-the-third-kind" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: criterion.com">[The Criterion Collection]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">criterion.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on criterion.com.</span></span></span>

The result was a spacecraft that was not simply seen but experienced. The lights create mystery because they do not clearly reveal the ship’s function. Viewers understand that something highly advanced is present, but the technology remains beyond explanation.

### The influence of industrial landscapes

The mothership’s unusual appearance also drew inspiration from earthly environments. The design has often been linked to the image of an illuminated industrial refinery, transforming familiar structures into something alien when viewed at night. This connection helped Spielberg achieve one of the film’s central ideas: the unknown does not need to be completely separate from human experience; it can emerge from ordinary things viewed in a new way.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Close_Encounters_of_the_Third_Kind" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Close Encounters of the Third Kind</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Close Encounters of the Third Kind</span></span></span>

That mixture of familiarity and strangeness is why the craft remains memorable. It does not look like a random fantasy object. It looks like a possible extension of architecture, engineering and civilisation — only from a world beyond Earth.

## The lasting influence on UFO imagery

The mothership of *Close Encounters of the [Third Kind]({{ 'third-kind/' | relative_url }})* became one of the most influential alien spacecraft designs in cinema because it expanded what a UFO could represent. After the film, many science-fiction stories moved away from purely hostile flying saucers and explored alien vessels as places of mystery, intelligence and cultural exchange.

Its influence can be seen in the way later films approached extraterrestrial technology. Rather than focusing only on invasion hardware, filmmakers increasingly imagined alien ships as environments with their own logic and history. The idea of a spacecraft as a “world” rather than just a vehicle became a recurring science-fiction concept.

The physical model itself has also become part of film history. The original mothership model is preserved at the Smithsonian’s National Air and Space Museum, where it remains an example of the practical effects craftsmanship that shaped pre-digital science fiction cinema.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.si.edu/object/yt_YYrwu8KvZA0" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: si.edu">[Smithsonian Institution]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Smithsonian InstitutionThe Close Encounters Mothership &#124; Smithsonian InstitutionMarch 19, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 19, 2025</span></span></span>

The model contains hidden details that were never central to the film’s story, including miniature objects placed by the artists as private jokes. These details reveal the human creativity behind the illusion: the alien machine was built by filmmakers who filled it with imagination and playfulness.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.si.edu/object/model-spacecraft-mother-ship-movie-close-encounters-third-kind%3Anasm_A19790906000" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: si.edu">[Smithsonian Institution]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on si.edu.</span></span></span>

## Why the mothership still defines alien contact

The lasting power of the *Close Encounters* mothership comes from a rare balance. It is enormous without being threatening, advanced without becoming cold, and mysterious without relying on horror. It represents a future in which contact with another intelligence might be an invitation to learn rather than a reason to fight.

In the history of [flying saucer imagery]({{ 'flying-saucer-imagery/' | relative_url }}), the film marked a major transformation. The saucer had once been a symbol of fear from the sky; Spielberg turned it into a symbol of hope from the stars. The mothership’s glowing arrival at Devil’s Tower remains one of science fiction’s clearest visual expressions of wonder — the moment when the unknown becomes something humanity wants to understand.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.criterion.com/current/posts/877-close-encounters-of-the-third-kind" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: criterion.com">[The Criterion Collection]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">criterion.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on criterion.com.</span></span></span>

    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
