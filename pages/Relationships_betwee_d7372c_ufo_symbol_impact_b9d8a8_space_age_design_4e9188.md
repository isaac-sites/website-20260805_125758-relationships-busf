## Introduction

[Flying saucer imagery]({{ 'flying-saucer-imagery/' | relative_url }}) did more than shape science fiction stories: it helped create a visual language for the Space Age itself. During the 1950s and 1960s, designers borrowed the smooth curves, hovering forms and futuristic associations of imagined spacecraft to create buildings, interiors and consumer products that suggested a technologically advanced tomorrow. These designs did not claim that alien craft were real; instead, they transformed [the flying saucer]({{ 'the-flying-saucer/' | relative_url }}) into a symbol of speed, progress and possibility.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Googie_architecture" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Googie architecture</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Googie architecture</span></span></span>

## Saucer forms in futuristic architecture

The strongest architectural influence of the flying saucer appeared in Googie architecture, a style that developed in post-war Southern California. Designed for a car-based society moving quickly along highways, Googie buildings used dramatic shapes intended to catch attention from a distance: exaggerated rooflines, sharp angles, neon signs, boomerangs and circular forms associated with spacecraft. Flying saucer imagery became one of several visual shortcuts for communicating “the future” to customers passing by.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Googie_architecture" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Googie architecture</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Googie architecture</span></span></span>

Coffee shops, petrol stations and roadside businesses became important showcases for this futuristic language. Their designs were not simply decorative; they reflected the belief that technology would reshape ordinary life. A building that looked like it had arrived from another planet suggested that the business inside belonged to a modern, efficient and exciting age. In this sense, the flying saucer became a commercial symbol of progress rather than only an object from [science fiction]({{ 'science-fiction/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Googie_architecture" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Googie architecture</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Googie architecture</span></span></span>

### When buildings began to look like spacecraft

Although many Space Age buildings borrowed from the same cultural atmosphere as [UFO imagery]({{ 'ufo-imagery/' | relative_url }}), not every futuristic structure was a direct copy of a saucer. Architects often translated the idea of flight into curved shells, floating forms and unusual silhouettes. The former TWA Flight Center at New York’s John F. Kennedy International Airport, designed by Eero Saarinen and opened in 1962, is a key example. Saarinen aimed to capture the excitement and “spirit of flight” through sweeping concrete forms and an interior that made passengers feel as though they were entering a machine designed for the future.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/stories/story/eero-saarinen-a-place-in-architectural-history/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Eero Saarinen: A Place in Architectural History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Eero Saarinen: A Place in Architectural History - CIADecember 21, 2017...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 21, 2017</span></span></span>

The TWA terminal shows an important difference between science-fiction imagery and real architecture. Its shape was not a literal flying saucer, but its organic curves, dramatic spaces and futuristic atmosphere belonged to the same cultural world that produced UFO-inspired design. It represented confidence in aviation, technology and global connection at a time when space exploration was becoming a defining symbol of modern life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/distributed/D/bo22381671.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: press.uchicago.edu">[University of Chicago Press]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">press.uchicago.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on uchicago.edu.</span></span></span>

## Technology optimism and visual style

The popularity of saucer-like forms reflected a broader Space Age belief that new technology could improve everyday experience. After the Second World War, rockets, satellites, nuclear energy and aviation created a sense that the future would be radically different from the past. Designers responded by replacing traditional rectangular forms with curves, domes and streamlined shapes that appeared more advanced and dynamic.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Googie_architecture" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Googie architecture</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Googie architecture</span></span></span>

Flying saucer imagery worked especially well because the shape itself communicated unfamiliar technology. A disc-shaped object had no obvious connection to existing vehicles such as cars or trains, making it ideal for representing something beyond ordinary human engineering. In consumer culture, the saucer became a visual promise: products, buildings and environments could appear as if they belonged to a future civilisation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Googie_architecture" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Googie architecture</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Googie architecture</span></span></span>

This influence appeared across interiors and industrial design as well. Designers experimented with plastics, moulded materials and rounded forms that suggested movement and flexibility. Furniture, lighting and household objects increasingly adopted sculptural shapes that matched the optimistic mood of the era. ## Legacy in modern design culture

The flying saucer’s influence declined as the optimism of the early Space Age gave way to more sceptical views of technology, but its visual language remained influential. Many Googie buildings were later demolished because their bold roadside style fell out of fashion, yet preservation efforts and renewed interest in mid-century design have restored appreciation for their futuristic ambition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Googie_architecture" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Googie architecture</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Googie architecture</span></span></span>

Today, Space Age design is often revisited because it represents a particular historical moment when the future seemed open-ended. Contemporary designers continue to use rounded forms, reflective surfaces and spacecraft-inspired aesthetics, not because they expect yesterday’s visions of tomorrow to become reality, but because those forms still communicate imagination and experimentation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.goodhousekeeping.com/home/decorating-ideas/a69632777/googie-most-searched-architecture-style-google/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: goodhousekeeping.com">[Good Housekeeping]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">goodhousekeeping.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">It&#x27;s heavily influenced by the Space Age and Atomic Era, featuring bright colors, neon lights, and dynamic shapes like boomerangs and fly...</span></span></span>

    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
