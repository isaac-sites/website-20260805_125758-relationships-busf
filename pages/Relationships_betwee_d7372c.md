## Introduction

UFOs and [science fiction]({{ 'science-fiction/' | relative_url }}) have shaped each other for more than a century. Science fiction provided many of the ideas, images and stories that influenced how people imagined visitors from other worlds, while modern [UFO reports]({{ 'ufo-reports/' | relative_url }}) in turn supplied writers and filmmakers with new material for alien encounters, spacecraft designs and narratives about humanity’s place in the universe. The relationship is not simply that fiction created belief or that sightings inspired fiction; it is a continuous exchange between speculation, technology, media and public imagination.

The modern UFO era emerged in the late 1940s, at a time when science fiction was already exploring rockets, alien civilisations and interplanetary travel. [Kenneth Arnold]({{ 'kenneth-arnold/' | relative_url }})’s 1947 report of unusual objects over Washington State helped popularise the phrase “flying saucer”, although his original description referred to their movement rather than a precise saucer shape. The media interpretation of his account helped create an image that soon became central to both UFO culture and science fiction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.history.com/articles/kenneth-arnold" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.com">[HISTORY]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kenneth Arnold</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kenneth Arnold - UFO, 1947 &amp; Flying Saucer &#124; HISTORYFebruary 22, 2010...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 22, 2010</span></span></span>

## How science fiction created the language of UFOs

Science fiction existed before the modern UFO phenomenon. Writers had imagined extraterrestrial life, alien invasions and advanced spacecraft for decades before the first widely publicised flying saucer reports. H. G. Wells’s 1898 novel *The War of the Worlds* presented a technologically superior alien invasion, helping establish themes that later appeared in UFO-related films and public discussions. The 1938 radio adaptation of the novel by Orson Welles also demonstrated how fictional alien scenarios could influence public reactions to imagined extraterrestrial threats.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.history.com/articles/kenneth-arnold" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.com">[HISTORY]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kenneth Arnold</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kenneth Arnold - UFO, 1947 &amp; Flying Saucer &#124; HISTORYFebruary 22, 2010...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 22, 2010</span></span></span>

Early science fiction magazines and novels helped create a visual vocabulary for space travel. Artists and writers popularised ideas such as metallic spacecraft, visitors from other planets and encounters between humans and technologically advanced beings. When reports of strange objects appeared in the 1940s and 1950s, many people interpreted them through this existing fictional framework.

The connection became especially strong during the Cold War. Concerns about nuclear weapons, secret military technology and possible invasion were reflected in both UFO reports and science fiction films. Movies such as *The Day the Earth Stood Still* (1951) and *The War of the Worlds* (1953) used alien visitors to explore political fears, including conflict between nations and anxieties about destructive technology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.history.com/articles/kenneth-arnold" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.com">[HISTORY]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kenneth Arnold</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kenneth Arnold - UFO, 1947 &amp; Flying Saucer &#124; HISTORYFebruary 22, 2010...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 22, 2010</span></span></span>

## How UFO reports changed science fiction

While science fiction influenced UFO imagery, reported UFO encounters also transformed science fiction storytelling. The classic disc-shaped spacecraft became one of the most recognisable symbols of alien visitation after the 1947 flying saucer wave. The image appeared repeatedly in films, television and literature, reinforcing the association between UFOs and extraterrestrial technology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.history.com/articles/kenneth-arnold" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.com">[HISTORY]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kenneth Arnold</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kenneth Arnold - UFO, 1947 &amp; Flying Saucer &#124; HISTORYFebruary 22, 2010...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 22, 2010</span></span></span>

The growth of UFO investigations gave writers and filmmakers new narrative material. The United States Air Force investigated thousands of UFO reports through projects including Project Sign, Project Grudge and Project Blue Book. Although these investigations did not establish evidence of extraterrestrial spacecraft, the official attention given to UFO reports helped make the subject a major part of popular culture. Project Blue Book collected 12,618 reports between 1952 and 1969, with most ultimately classified as identifiable phenomena and a smaller number remaining unresolved because of insufficient information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/news/articles/project-blue-book-50th-anniversary" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesPublic Interest in UFOs Persists 50 Years After Project Blue Book Termination &#124; National ArchivesDecember 5, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 5, 2019</span></span></span>

Science fiction increasingly moved beyond simple invasion stories. Instead of portraying aliens only as enemies, later works explored communication, misunderstanding and the possibility of peaceful contact. This shift reflected changing ideas about extraterrestrial intelligence and humanity’s relationship with the unknown.

## The rise of the “close encounter” idea

One of the clearest examples of UFO culture and science fiction influencing each other is the concept of the “close encounter”. Astronomer J. Allen Hynek, who worked as a scientific consultant on US Air Force UFO investigations, developed a classification system for different types of reported encounters. His 1972 book *The UFO Experience* introduced categories including “Close Encounter of the [Third Kind]({{ 'third-kind/' | relative_url }})”, describing reports involving apparent occupants.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cufos.org/hynek-biography/j-allen-hynek-biography/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufos.org">[Center for UFO Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Center for UFO Studies J. Allen Hynek Biography</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Center for UFO StudiesJ. Allen Hynek Biography - Center for UFO Studies...</span></span></span>

Steven Spielberg’s 1977 film *Close Encounters of the Third Kind* brought this terminology to a global audience. The film used ideas drawn from UFO research, and Hynek served as a scientific adviser, appearing briefly in the film. Rather than focusing on invasion, it presented contact as a mysterious but potentially positive event, helping shift popular portrayals of aliens from threats towards possible communication partners.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.encyclopedia.com/literature-and-arts/performing-arts/film-and-television/close-encounters-third-kind" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: encyclopedia.com">[Encyclopedia.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">encyclopedia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Close Encounters Of The Third Kind &#124; Encyclopedia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">May 17, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 17, 2018</span></span></span>

This approach influenced later science fiction, including stories about diplomacy, alien psychology and the challenges of communicating with a civilisation unlike our own.

## Where UFO reality and science fiction diverge

Despite their close cultural relationship, UFO reports and science fiction remain different categories. Science fiction is deliberately imaginative: it explores possibilities without needing evidence that those possibilities exist. UFO investigations, by contrast, attempt to determine whether reported observations have conventional explanations, require further study or remain unresolved.

Many UFO reports have been explained through ordinary causes such as aircraft, atmospheric effects, astronomical objects or classified technology. Official investigations, including Project Blue Book, did not find evidence that UFO reports represented extraterrestrial spacecraft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/news/articles/project-blue-book-50th-anniversary" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesPublic Interest in UFOs Persists 50 Years After Project Blue Book Termination &#124; National ArchivesDecember 5, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 5, 2019</span></span></span>

The continuing overlap occurs because both fields address similar human questions: Are we alone? How would humanity react to a more advanced civilisation? Could unknown technology appear indistinguishable from magic? Science fiction explores these questions through storytelling, while UFO culture explores them through reported experiences and investigations.

## Why the relationship remains influential today

The relationship between UFOs and science fiction continues because both are expressions of curiosity about the unknown. Modern science fiction often reflects contemporary UFO debates, while UFO discussions frequently borrow concepts developed through fiction, including advanced propulsion, alien civilisations and hidden technology.

Recent interest in unidentified anomalous phenomena (UAP), the newer term often used in official discussions instead of UFO, has renewed public attention, but scientific interest remains focused on evidence-based investigation rather than assuming extraterrestrial explanations. Researchers studying UAP emphasise careful analysis of observations, data quality and alternative explanations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.06794" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv The New Science of Unidentified Aerospace-Undersea Phenomena (UAP</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The New Science of Unidentified Aerospace-Undersea Phenomena (UAP)January 27, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 27, 2025</span></span></span>

The lasting connection between UFOs and science fiction is therefore less about proving fictional ideas true and more about how both areas shape human imagination. Together they provide a cultural space for exploring technological change, exploration beyond Earth and the possibility of encounters with forms of life or intelligence unlike anything humanity has known.
