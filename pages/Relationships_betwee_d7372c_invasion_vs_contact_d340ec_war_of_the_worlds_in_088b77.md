## Introduction

H. G. Wells’s *[The War]({{ 'the-war/' | relative_url }}) of the Worlds* (1898) transformed the alien invasion story by turning extraterrestrial arrival into a nightmare of colonial conquest. Instead of imagining Mars as a source of friendly discovery, Wells reversed the direction of imperial expansion: Britain, one of the world’s dominant colonial powers, becomes the invaded territory. The Martians arrive with overwhelming technology, treat humans as an inferior species, and expose the vulnerability of a civilisation that assumes its own superiority.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.loc.gov/item/24022215" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Library of Congress The war of the worlds, &#124; Library of Congress</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Library of Congress The war of the worlds, &#124; Library of Congress</span></span></span>

The novel’s lasting importance within [science fiction]({{ 'science-fiction/' | relative_url }}) is not only that it created a template for hostile alien contact, but that it made invasion itself a moral and political question. The Martians are frightening because their behaviour resembles the logic of empire: advanced powers taking resources, dismissing local life and using technology to dominate weaker societies. Wells’s story therefore connects UFO and alien-invasion fiction with historical fears about imperialism, technological inequality and the consequences of assuming that power guarantees safety.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://zancojournal.su.edu.krd/index.php/JAHS/en/article/view/713" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: zancojournal.su.edu.krd">[Zanco Journal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">zancojournal.su.edu.krd</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Zanco JournalA New Historicist Reading of Reverse Colonization in H. ## Martian invasion and colonial parallels

### Britain becomes the colonised world

When Wells published *The War of the Worlds*, the British Empire was at its height, controlling territories across Africa, Asia, the Pacific and elsewhere. The novel’s central imaginative reversal was to place Britain in the position normally occupied by colonised peoples: a technologically outmatched society confronted by a stronger outside force. Literary scholars have often described this as a form of “reverse colonisation”, where the fears associated with imperial expansion are redirected towards the imperial centre itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://zancojournal.su.edu.krd/index.php/JAHS/en/article/view/713" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: zancojournal.su.edu.krd">[Zanco Journal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">zancojournal.su.edu.krd</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Zanco JournalA New Historicist Reading of Reverse Colonization in H. G. Wells’ The War of the Worlds &#124; Zanco Journal of Human SciencesSep...</span></span></span>

The opening of the novel establishes this reversal by challenging human confidence in its own dominance. Humanity believes it has mastered Earth, but Wells imagines another civilisation viewing humans in the same way humans have viewed supposedly “lower” forms of life. The Martians’ arrival forces readers to question whether human ideas of progress and civilisation are universal or simply expressions of power.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.penguinrandomhouse.ca/books/47384/the-war-of-the-worlds-by-hg-wells/excerpt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: penguinrandomhouse.ca">[Penguin Random House Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">penguinrandomhouse.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on penguinrandomhouse.ca.</span></span></span>

The comparison is made explicit when the narrator reflects on human history. Wells refers to the destruction of animals and the treatment of Indigenous peoples, including the Tasmanians, arguing that humanity cannot easily condemn Martian violence without recognising its own record of conquest.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.penguinrandomhouse.ca/books/47384/the-war-of-the-worlds-by-hg-wells/excerpt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: penguinrandomhouse.ca">[Penguin Random House Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">penguinrandomhouse.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on penguinrandomhouse.ca.</span></span></span> This passage has become one of the most discussed elements of the novel because it reveals both Wells’s anti-imperial criticism and the limitations of the language used by a late nineteenth-century writer.

### The Martians as imperial conquerors

The Martians do not arrive as explorers seeking communication. Their mission resembles a colonial campaign: they establish a foothold, deploy superior weapons and rapidly destroy human resistance. Their attitude towards Earth mirrors the assumption that technologically advanced societies have the right to exploit less powerful ones.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/13528165.2023.2272513" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tandfonline.com">[Taylor &amp; Francis Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tandfonline.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on tandfonline.com.</span></span></span>

The alien invasion framework works because it removes the familiar justifications often used in imperial histories. The Martians do not claim to be bringing civilisation, trade or progress. They simply demonstrate overwhelming force. By making humans the victims of the same logic of domination, Wells exposes the brutality behind conquest narratives.

This idea became a foundation for later alien-invasion fiction. Many stories that followed *The War of the Worlds* continued to use extraterrestrial enemies as symbols of invasion, occupation or the fear of losing political independence. The alien became a way to discuss human conflicts without directly reproducing them.

## Technology gaps and human vulnerability

The terror of Wells’s invasion comes from the imbalance between human and [Martian technology]({{ 'alien-technology/' | relative_url }}). The Martians possess fighting machines, heat rays and other advanced tools that make conventional military responses almost useless. Human armies are not defeated because they lack courage; they are defeated because they are operating against a civilisation with a completely different level of technological capability.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.encyclopedia.com/arts/educational-magazines/war-worlds" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: encyclopedia.com">[Encyclopedia.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">encyclopedia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The War of the Worlds &#124; Encyclopedia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The War of the Worlds &#124; Encyclopedia.com</span></span></span>

### The machine as a symbol of imperial power

The Martian tripod became one of science fiction’s defining images because it combines alien biology with industrial warfare. It represents a future in which technology has expanded the ability of a powerful society to control and destroy. The machines are not merely weapons; they are instruments of occupation.

This reflected late Victorian concerns about modern warfare and industrial competition. The nineteenth century had seen rapid advances in weapons, transport and communication, allowing European empires to project power across vast distances. Wells transformed those realities into a cosmic scenario: what if another civilisation possessed an even greater technological advantage and used it against humanity?<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.iarconsortium.org/iarjel/26/113/critiquing-imperialism-and-its-effects-on-humanity-in-science-fiction-with-reference-to-h-g-wells-s-the-war-of-the-worlds-857/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: iarconsortium.org">[IAR Consortium]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">iarconsortium.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on iarconsortium.org.</span></span></span>

The result was an inversion of the usual imperial adventure story. Victorian fiction often portrayed Europeans travelling into unknown territories with superior tools and knowledge. Wells instead imagines Europeans encountering an outside force whose technology makes their own achievements appear primitive.

### The collapse of confidence

A key fear in *The War of the Worlds* is psychological as much as military. The invasion destroys the belief that human civilisation is secure. People who previously saw themselves as masters of nature discover that they can become powerless almost instantly.

This vulnerability became one of the defining features of alien invasion fiction. Later stories used similar ideas to explore fears about nuclear weapons, global conflict and technological change. The alien threat often represents not only an enemy from space but the possibility that humanity’s own confidence in progress may be misplaced.

## Legacy of the invasion template

### From colonial anxiety to modern alien warfare

*The War of the Worlds* established many conventions that still shape alien-invasion stories: the sudden arrival of outsiders, the inability of existing institutions to respond, the use of advanced technology by an unknown enemy and the question of whether humanity can survive contact with a superior civilisation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.loc.gov/item/24022215" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Library of Congress The war of the worlds, &#124; Library of Congress</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Library of Congress The war of the worlds, &#124; Library of Congress</span></span></span>

However, Wells’s most important contribution was not simply the idea of aliens attacking Earth. Earlier fiction had imagined conflicts with beings from other worlds, but Wells connected alien invasion with historical experiences of empire. His Martians are frightening because they behave according to patterns humans already recognise from their own history.

The story therefore sits at the foundation of a broader science-fiction debate about whether [first contact]({{ 'first-contact/' | relative_url }}) should be imagined as conquest or communication. In later works, writers would increasingly challenge the assumption that alien arrival must mean conflict, creating stories focused on diplomacy, understanding and peaceful exchange. Yet the invasion model remains powerful because Wells identified a deep human fear: that contact with a more advanced civilisation might reveal that humanity is not the dominant force it believes itself to be.

### Why the colonial reading remains influential

The colonial interpretation of *The War of the Worlds* remains widely discussed because it explains why the novel feels more complex than a simple monster story. The Martians are not merely villains from another planet; they are a mirror reflecting the practices of powerful societies on Earth.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://zancojournal.su.edu.krd/index.php/JAHS/en/article/view/713" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: zancojournal.su.edu.krd">[Zanco Journal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">zancojournal.su.edu.krd</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Zanco JournalA New Historicist Reading of Reverse Colonization in H. G. Wells’ The War of the Worlds &#124; Zanco Journal of Human SciencesSep...</span></span></span>

At the same time, the novel is not a straightforward historical allegory with only one meaning. It also explores scientific uncertainty, survival, evolution and the dangers of assuming that intelligence or technology makes a civilisation morally superior. Its enduring influence comes from combining these ideas into a single encounter: an alien invasion that is simultaneously a war story, a colonial reversal and a warning about human arrogance.

      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
