## Introduction

Steven Spielberg’s *Close Encounters of the [Third Kind]({{ 'third-kind/' | relative_url }})* (1977) changed the visual language of UFO contact. Instead of presenting alien visitors mainly as invaders or monsters, the film offered a different expectation: contact as a mysterious but potentially meaningful meeting marked by lights, communication and personal transformation. Its influence came not from proving anything about UFOs, but from giving audiences a powerful set of images through which later reports of strange lights and possible encounters were interpreted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/liverpool-scholarship-online/book/37761/chapter-abstract/332135354" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicClose Encounters: Cultural Impact &#124; Close Encounters of the Third Kind &#124; Liverpool Scholarship Online &#124; Oxford AcademicSeptem...</span></span></span>

## Film imagery and public imagination

### How Spielberg changed the expected UFO encounter

Before *Close Encounters*, many popular [science fiction]({{ 'science-fiction/' | relative_url }}) stories portrayed extraterrestrials through conflict. Alien visitors were often threats, invaders or symbols of technological danger. Spielberg’s film shifted attention towards the emotional and psychological experience of meeting something beyond humanity. The central drama is not a battle against aliens but the desire to understand them, communicate with them and discover what contact might mean.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/liverpool-scholarship-online/book/37761/chapter-abstract/332135354" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicClose Encounters: Cultural Impact &#124; Close Encounters of the Third Kind &#124; Liverpool Scholarship Online &#124; Oxford AcademicSeptem...</span></span></span>

Several images from the film became lasting reference points for [UFO culture]({{ 'ufo-culture/' | relative_url }}):


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--checklist" markdown="1">

* **Unusual lights in the sky:** The film’s opening sequences and later arrival scene reinforced the association between UFOs and dramatic nocturnal lights. A strange glow in the distance became a cinematic sign of possible contact.
* **Communication through patterns and signals:** The musical five-note exchange between humans and the visitors presented contact as a process of learning rather than a simple encounter with a superior force.
* **The human transformation story:** Characters are not merely witnesses to an event; they are changed by it. Roy Neary’s obsession after his sighting turns the UFO experience into a personal search for meaning.

</div>

These images influenced later fictional portrayals and helped establish expectations about what a “real” encounter might look like. Cultural studies of the film have highlighted its continuing effect on science fiction, UFO subcultures and public fascination with [extraterrestrial life]({{ 'extraterrestrial-life/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/liverpool-scholarship-online/book/37761/chapter-abstract/332135354" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicClose Encounters: Cultural Impact &#124; Close Encounters of the Third Kind &#124; Liverpool Scholarship Online &#124; Oxford AcademicSeptem...</span></span></span>

The importance of the film was also its timing. Released in 1977 during a period of strong public interest in space exploration and [UFO reports]({{ 'ufo-reports/' | relative_url }}), it offered a hopeful alternative to darker alien narratives. The possibility of extraterrestrial contact was presented as an event that could expand human understanding rather than simply create fear.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://time.com/5015537/close-encounters-of-the-third-kind-1977/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: time.com">[TIME]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">time.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Extreme secrecy surrounded its production to maintain the element of surprise. ## From Hynek’s term to popular language

### A scientific classification becomes a cultural phrase

The phrase “close encounter” did not originate in Hollywood. Hynek developed his classification system while working as an astronomical consultant on U.S. Air Force UFO investigations, including Project Blue Book. His categories were intended as a way of organising reports, not as proof that extraterrestrial explanations were correct. A close encounter of the first kind involved a nearby sighting, the second kind involved reported physical effects, and the third kind involved claimed observation of occupants.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tcm.com/articles/467312/the-big-idea-close-encounters-of-the-third-kind" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tcm.com">[Turner Classic Movies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tcm.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Turner Classic Movies The Big Idea</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Turner Classic MoviesThe Big Idea - Close Encounters of the Third KindDecember 30, 2011...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 30, 2011</span></span></span>

*Close Encounters of the Third Kind* transformed this technical classification into a widely understood cultural phrase. The film’s title encouraged audiences to associate “third kind” with direct interaction between humans and alien beings, even though Hynek’s original system was a classification of reports rather than a prediction of alien contact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tcm.com/articles/467315/pop-culture-101-close-encounters-of-the-third-kind" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tcm.com">[Turner Classic Movies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tcm.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Turner Classic Movies Pop Culture 101</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Turner Classic MoviesPop Culture 101 - Close Encounters of the Third Kind...</span></span></span>

This shift illustrates how science fiction can reshape the meaning of scientific or specialist language. Once the film became familiar, people could describe unusual experiences using a ready-made narrative structure. A person who saw unexplained lights might no longer describe only an observation; popular culture had supplied a vocabulary of “encounters”, “contact” and possible communication.

Hynek’s involvement added to the film’s cultural authority. He served as a consultant and appeared briefly in the movie, creating a connection between a real UFO investigator and a fictional representation of contact. That association helped blur the boundary between UFO research, speculation and entertainment in public discussions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tcm.com/articles/467312/the-big-idea-close-encounters-of-the-third-kind" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tcm.com">[Turner Classic Movies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tcm.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Turner Classic Movies The Big Idea</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Turner Classic MoviesThe Big Idea - Close Encounters of the Third KindDecember 30, 2011...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 30, 2011</span></span></span>

## Real sightings viewed through movie ideas

### When familiar images shape interpretation

The relationship between *Close Encounters* and UFO expectations is most visible when people interpret ambiguous experiences through familiar cultural patterns. A distant light, unusual movement or unexplained event does not arrive with its meaning attached. People use existing ideas to make sense of uncertainty, and popular media can provide some of those ideas.

The film did not create UFO reports, and it cannot explain why individuals report unusual experiences. However, it contributed to a shared visual framework: glowing objects, silent observation, attempts at communication and transformative personal experiences. Later UFO stories have often been discussed using language and imagery that resemble this framework.

This influence is especially important because many UFO reports begin with incomplete information. Witnesses may see lights at night, unusual shapes or events that are difficult to identify. The cultural images supplied by films can affect which explanations feel familiar or imaginable. A mysterious light may be understood simply as an aircraft, atmospheric phenomenon or unknown object, but the same sight can also be interpreted through the idea of a possible encounter.

### The difference between influence and evidence

[The cultural impact]({{ 'the-cultural-impact/' | relative_url }}) of *Close Encounters of the Third Kind* does not mean that reports inspired by similar imagery are fictional or that every account can be explained by media influence. Rather, the film demonstrates a broader process: human interpretations of unexplained events are shaped by stories, symbols and expectations already present in society.

For UFO researchers, historians and media scholars, this distinction is central. The existence of a popular image does not determine whether an individual sighting has a conventional explanation, remains unresolved or represents something else. What the film clearly changed was the language available for discussing such experiences.

By turning UFO contact into a story of communication and discovery, *Close Encounters of the Third Kind* became one of the strongest examples of science fiction shaping public expectations about possible extraterrestrial encounters.
