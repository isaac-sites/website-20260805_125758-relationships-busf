## Introduction

*Arrival* (2016) presents alien translation as a problem that cannot be solved by a simple dictionary or futuristic translator. The film asks a more difficult question: can humans understand a species whose way of organising meaning may be fundamentally different from our own? Through linguist Louise Banks’ attempt to communicate with the heptapods, *Arrival* turns [first contact]({{ 'first-contact/' | relative_url }}) into a study of language, perception and trust.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.utoronto.ca/news/communicating-aliens-u-t-linguists-talk-facts-behind-film-arrival" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: utoronto.ca">[University of Toronto]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">utoronto.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of TorontoCommunicating with aliens? ## The heptapod communication problem

### Why alien translation cannot start with words

When the heptapods arrive on Earth, humans face a problem familiar to real linguists studying unknown languages: there is no shared vocabulary, grammar or cultural context. Louise cannot begin by asking what a particular alien sound means because she does not yet know whether the sounds represent objects, actions, ideas or something entirely different.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.utoronto.ca/news/communicating-aliens-u-t-linguists-talk-facts-behind-film-arrival" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: utoronto.ca">[University of Toronto]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">utoronto.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of TorontoCommunicating with aliens? U of T linguists talk facts behind the film &#x27;Arrival&#x27; &#124; University of TorontoNovember 25...</span></span></span>

The film’s solution is based on a principle from real-world field linguistics: communication begins with establishing basic references. Louise uses objects, gestures and repeated interactions to connect concepts with meanings. Instead of assuming that the aliens think like humans, she treats their language as a system that must be observed and analysed. This reflects how linguists approach undocumented human languages, where researchers must build understanding through careful comparison rather than direct translation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://jessica.lingspace.org/arrival/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jessica.lingspace.org">[Jessica Coon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jessica.lingspace.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Jessica Coon -Arrival: Linguistic fieldwork on the big screen</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Jessica Coon...</span></span></span>

The heptapods’ written language makes the challenge even greater. Their circular symbols, known as logograms, do not function like ordinary human writing. Rather than arranging letters or words in a simple sequence, the symbols represent complete ideas and are presented as connected circular forms. The film’s design team created hundreds of these symbols and developed rules for how their meanings could be represented visually.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2016/11/arrivals-designers-crafted-mesmerizing-alien-alphabet/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arrivals designers crafted mesmerizing alien alphabet</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">How Arrival&#x27;s Designers Crafted a Mesmerizing Alien Alphabet &#124; WIREDNovember 16, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 16, 2016</span></span></span>

This design choice supports one of *Arrival*’s major arguments: a truly [alien language]({{ 'alien-language/' | relative_url }}) might not simply be another version of a human language. It might reflect a different understanding of structure, time and meaning itself.

## Language and changing perspectives

### The Sapir–Whorf idea in science-fiction form

The most ambitious part of *Arrival* is its suggestion that learning a new language can change how someone experiences reality. The film draws on the linguistic relativity debate, often associated with the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis, which explores whether the language people use influences how they perceive and organise the world. Modern linguistics generally rejects the strongest version of the idea — that language completely determines thought — but accepts that language can influence attention, categorisation and habitual ways of thinking.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.simplypsychology.org/sapir-whorf-hypothesis.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: simplypsychology.org">[Simply Psychology]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">simplypsychology.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Simply Psychology Sapir–Whorf Hypothesis (Linguistic Relativity Hypothesis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Simply PsychologySapir–Whorf Hypothesis (Linguistic Relativity Hypothesis)May 18, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 18, 2026</span></span></span>

In *Arrival*, this concept is pushed into speculative territory. Louise’s increasing understanding of the heptapods’ written language changes her perception of time itself. The film imagines language not simply as a tool for communication but as a cognitive framework capable of reshaping consciousness.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/book/31973/chapter-abstract/267717021" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicThe linguistics of Arrival: Heptapods, field linguistics, and Universal Grammar &#124; Language Invention in Linguistics Pedagogy...</span></span></span>

This idea connects *Arrival* to a broader tradition of science fiction that uses alien encounters to question human assumptions. The aliens are not presented as enemies who must be defeated or mysteries that must be solved through technology. Instead, they challenge humanity’s belief that human categories are universal.

### Heptapod language as a different model of reality

The heptapods’ language is unusual because their written communication reflects a non-linear view of events. Human writing normally unfolds in a sequence: one word follows another, and one event follows another. The heptapods’ circular symbols suggest a form of communication where the beginning and ending of an idea are understood together.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.engadget.com/2016-11-14-dissecting-the-alien-language-in-arrival.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: engadget.com">[Engadget]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">engadget.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dissecting the alien language in &#x27;Arrival</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dissecting the alien language in &#x27;Arrival</span></span></span>

This makes translation a philosophical problem as much as a technical one. Louise is not only asking, “What does this symbol mean?” She is confronting a deeper question: what happens when the structure of another intelligence’s language does not match the structure of human experience?

The film therefore presents translation as an act of perspective-taking. Understanding another species requires humans to recognise that their own assumptions about meaning, time and communication may be limited.

## Trust during first contact

### Translation as a way to prevent conflict

A major tension in *Arrival* comes from the gap between understanding and suspicion. Governments and military leaders are not only trying to decode the heptapods’ language; they are trying to decide whether the aliens are a threat. A misunderstanding could have consequences because words exchanged during first contact are interpreted through fear and uncertainty.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/story/arrival-science-fact-fiction/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arrival science fact fiction</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The science of Arrival: what the film got right (and wrong) &#124; WIREDNovember 12, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 12, 2016</span></span></span>

Louise’s approach differs from the more defensive responses around her. She argues that communication must come before conclusions. Her work shows that translation is not merely a technical service provided after a decision has been made; it shapes the decision itself.

The film highlights a real issue in cross-cultural communication: a message cannot be separated from the assumptions of the people receiving it. The same phrase, gesture or symbol can carry different meanings depending on context. *Arrival* expands this human problem to an encounter between entirely different species.

### The importance of patient interpretation

The film’s most realistic contribution is not the idea of instant alien understanding but the opposite: meaningful communication would probably require time. Linguists consulted on *Arrival* noted that the film’s emphasis on fieldwork, gradual discovery and uncertainty reflects important aspects of real linguistic research.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ciol.org.uk/civicrm/event/info?id=193&amp;reset=1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ciol.org.uk">[CIOL]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ciol.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ciol.org.uk.</span></span></span>

Louise does not “crack” the alien language like a code. She learns it through repeated interaction, testing hypotheses and revising her understanding. This makes *Arrival* unusual among UFO and [alien-contact stories]({{ 'alien-contact-stories/' | relative_url }}) because the central achievement is not defeating an alien threat or discovering [alien technology]({{ 'alien-technology/' | relative_url }}). It is learning how to listen.

## Why *Arrival* changed the alien translation question

Before *Arrival*, many fictional first-contact stories treated translation as a background convenience. Characters might activate a [universal translator]({{ 'universal-translators/' | relative_url }}) and move directly to diplomacy or conflict. Denis Villeneuve’s film instead places translation at the centre of the narrative, making language the main technology of first contact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/story/arrival-science-fact-fiction/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arrival science fact fiction</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The science of Arrival: what the film got right (and wrong) &#124; WIREDNovember 12, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 12, 2016</span></span></span>

Its lasting contribution to science fiction is the idea that the greatest barrier between civilisations may not be distance or technology, but interpretation. If humanity ever encountered another intelligent species, the first challenge would not necessarily be asking what aliens want. It would be discovering whether humans and aliens can create enough shared meaning to ask the question at all.
