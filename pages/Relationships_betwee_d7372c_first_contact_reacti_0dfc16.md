## Introduction

Imagined [first contact]({{ 'first-contact/' | relative_url }}) with extraterrestrial intelligence has become one of [science fiction]({{ 'science-fiction/' | relative_url }})’s most powerful ways of exploring human psychology and social behaviour. Stories about UFOs and alien visitors are rarely only about aliens; they are about how humans respond when confronted with something that challenges assumptions about safety, identity, religion, politics, science and humanity’s place in the universe. ## Why imagined contact creates such strong reactions

A first-contact scenario combines two powerful human experiences: curiosity about the unknown and anxiety about losing control. An alien civilisation represents both a scientific opportunity and a challenge to familiar ideas about human uniqueness. Science fiction often uses this tension to ask whether humanity would respond with cooperation, conflict or confusion.

The imagined arrival of an extraterrestrial intelligence also forces questions about authority. Who would speak for Earth? Would governments control information, would scientists lead communication, or would public opinion shape events? These questions are not limited to fiction. Organisations involved in the search for extraterrestrial intelligence (SETI) have developed principles for how possible discoveries should be verified and communicated, recognising that even an initial detection could have major social consequences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://iaaseti.org/en/declaration-principles-concerning-activities-following-detection/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: iaaseti.org">[IAASeti]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">iaaseti.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Declaration of Principles Concerning Activities Following the Detection of Extraterrestrial Intelligence...</span></span></span>

Researchers studying possible [contact scenarios]({{ 'contact-scenarios/' | relative_url }}) have noted that outcomes would depend heavily on the type of encounter imagined. A distant radio signal, a message from an advanced civilisation, and a physical arrival on Earth would produce very different human responses. Discussions often reveal more about human hopes and fears than about the hypothetical visitors themselves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1104.4462" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Would contact with extraterrestrials benefit or harm humanity? ## Fear, confusion and the power of realistic storytelling

One of the most famous examples of human reaction to imagined first contact came from Orson Welles’s 1938 radio adaptation of H. G. Wells’s *The War of the Worlds*. The programme presented a fictional [Martian invasion]({{ 'martian-invasion/' | relative_url }}) through the style of emergency news reporting. Some listeners who missed the introduction understood the drama as a real event and reacted with alarm. Reports described people contacting authorities and seeking information about the supposed attack.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/war-of-the-worlds-behind-the-panic" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalgeographic.com">[National Geographic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalgeographic.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">war of the worlds behind the panic</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Geographic&#x27;War of the Worlds&#x27;: Behind the 1938 Radio Show Panic &#124; National GeographicJune 17, 2005...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 17, 2005</span></span></span>

The event is often remembered as a nationwide panic, although later historical research has argued that the scale of the reaction was exaggerated. Studies of the broadcast suggest that genuine confusion occurred among some listeners, but the idea of widespread mass hysteria was amplified by contemporary media coverage and later retellings. The more important lesson for understanding human reactions is not that people universally panic, but that realistic communication formats can strongly influence how audiences interpret extraordinary claims.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_War_of_the_Worlds_%281938_radio_drama%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The War of the Worlds (1938 radio drama</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The War of the Worlds (1938 radio drama</span></span></span>

The broadcast became an early example of how science-fiction scenarios can move beyond entertainment and affect public thinking about real-world possibilities. It demonstrated that people respond not only to the idea of alien contact itself, but also to the credibility of the messenger, the surrounding political atmosphere and the information environment in which the event occurs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/war-of-the-worlds-behind-the-panic" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalgeographic.com">[National Geographic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalgeographic.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">war of the worlds behind the panic</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Geographic&#x27;War of the Worlds&#x27;: Behind the 1938 Radio Show Panic &#124; National GeographicJune 17, 2005...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 17, 2005</span></span></span>

## From invasion fears to hopes of communication

Early [alien-contact stories]({{ 'alien-contact-stories/' | relative_url }}) often presented extraterrestrials as threats. During the twentieth century, many science-fiction narratives reflected fears about war, invasion and technological destruction. However, other works gradually shifted towards communication and mutual understanding, presenting first contact as a test of human maturity rather than simply a military crisis.

The 1951 film *The Day the Earth Stood Still* is a key example. Instead of portraying an alien visitor only as an attacker, it imagined contact as a warning about humanity’s use of dangerous technology and its inability to cooperate. The film used an extraterrestrial encounter to examine Cold War fears about nuclear weapons and global conflict.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/1005371-day_the_earth_stood_still" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rottentomatoes.com">[Rotten Tomatoes]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rottentomatoes.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Rotten Tomatoes The Day the Earth Stood Still &#124; Rotten Tomatoes</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Rotten Tomatoes The Day the Earth Stood Still &#124; Rotten Tomatoes</span></span></span>

## Scientists, governments and the question of who responds

Imagined first contact often becomes a debate about expertise and trust. Science-fiction stories frequently depict governments attempting to control information, while scientists argue for careful investigation and open communication. These fictional conflicts reflect real discussions about how humanity should respond to a possible detection of extraterrestrial intelligence.

SETI researchers have developed international guidance stating that potential signals should be carefully verified before public announcements are made. The reason is not only scientific accuracy but also social responsibility: a false alarm or poorly interpreted signal could create unnecessary confusion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://iaaseti.org/en/declaration-principles-concerning-activities-following-detection/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: iaaseti.org">[IAASeti]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">iaaseti.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Declaration of Principles Concerning Activities Following the Detection of Extraterrestrial Intelligence...</span></span></span>

At the same time, the search for extraterrestrial intelligence is influenced by public imagination. Science fiction has helped popularise ideas about alien civilisations while also creating assumptions that scientists must separate from evidence. Researchers have noted that fictional portrayals can shape how society understands space exploration, including expectations about discovery, communication and humanity’s future.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05318" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visions of Human Futures in Space and SETIAugust 17, 2017...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 17, 2017</span></span></span>

## The human emotions behind first-contact stories

Across different eras, science fiction repeatedly returns to a small set of emotional responses:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Wonder:** Contact represents the possibility that humanity is not alone and that the universe contains forms of life beyond current understanding.
* **Fear:** Alien visitors can symbolise invasion, loss of control or the consequences of encountering a more advanced civilisation.
* **Hope:** Some stories imagine contact as a chance for humanity to overcome divisions and see itself as part of a larger cosmic community.
* **Suspicion:** Narratives about secrecy and misinformation reflect concerns about whether institutions would reveal the truth.
* **Self-reflection:** Many first-contact stories use aliens as a mirror, asking what humanity values and how it behaves when confronted with difference.

</div>

These reactions explain why UFOs and science fiction remain closely connected. The alien visitor is often less important than the human response: the arguments, emotions and social changes that follow the possibility of discovering another intelligence.

The enduring appeal of first-contact stories comes from their ability to turn an astronomical question into a human one. Whether the imagined visitors are peaceful explorers, mysterious signals or hostile invaders, the central drama usually concerns humanity’s ability to adapt.

Science fiction therefore acts as a rehearsal space for possible futures. It allows societies to explore difficult questions before they become real decisions: how evidence should be judged, how information should be shared and whether humans can respond to the unknown with cooperation rather than fear.
