## Introduction

[Science fiction]({{ 'science-fiction/' | relative_url }}) artwork helped create many of the UFO expectations that audiences later carried into films and popular culture. Long before cinema could convincingly show alien spacecraft, magazine covers, paperback illustrations and astronomical paintings taught readers what an encounter with visitors from another world might look like: metallic ships hovering above landscapes, strange lights cutting through dark skies, and machines whose designs suggested technology far beyond human engineering. These images did not simply decorate stories; they created a shared visual vocabulary that filmmakers later adapted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://societyillustrators.org/award-winners/frankrpaul/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: societyillustrators.org">[Society of Illustrators]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">societyillustrators.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Society of IllustratorsFrank R. ## Magazine illustrations and paperback covers

### Frank R. Paul and the invention of a visual future

One of the most important figures in shaping science fiction’s visual language was Frank R. Paul, whose cover illustrations for magazines including *[Amazing Stories]({{ 'amazing-stories/' | relative_url }})* helped define what imagined space technology looked like before modern spacecraft existed. The magazine, launched in 1926, gave artists a platform to create images of interplanetary travel, alien environments and futuristic machines for a mass audience.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://societyillustrators.org/award-winners/frankrpaul/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: societyillustrators.org">[Society of Illustrators]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">societyillustrators.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Society of IllustratorsFrank R. Paul – Hall of Fame 2025 - Society of Illustrators...</span></span></span>

Paul’s artwork often combined technical detail with dramatic spectacle. His spacecraft were usually large, brightly lit machines with clear mechanical features, designed to communicate both wonder and scientific possibility. These illustrations encouraged readers to imagine alien vehicles not as vague supernatural objects, but as engineered systems with recognizable structures, controls and propulsion methods.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://societyillustrators.org/award-winners/frankrpaul/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: societyillustrators.org">[Society of Illustrators]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">societyillustrators.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Society of IllustratorsFrank R. Paul – Hall of Fame 2025 - Society of Illustrators...</span></span></span>

Some of the visual ideas associated with UFOs appeared in science fiction art before they became dominant in public reports of unexplained objects. A 1929 cover of *Science Wonder Stories* illustrated by Paul is frequently discussed as an early example of a flying-saucer-like design, showing that circular or disc-shaped spacecraft already existed within speculative art traditions before the term “flying saucer” became widely popular in the late 1940s.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AScience_Wonder_Stories_Nov_1929_-_flying_saucer.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: commons.wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">commons.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">File:Science Wonder Stories Nov 1929 flying saucer.jpg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia CommonsFile:Science Wonder Stories Nov 1929 - flying saucer.jpg - Wikimedia Commons...</span></span></span>

This does not mean that artwork alone created later [UFO reports]({{ 'ufo-reports/' | relative_url }}), but it demonstrates an important cultural process: artists supplied audiences with ready-made ways of imagining unfamiliar objects. When people encountered descriptions of strange aerial phenomena, science fiction imagery provided a familiar visual framework for interpreting them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AScience_Wonder_Stories_Nov_1929_-_flying_saucer.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: commons.wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">commons.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">File:Science Wonder Stories Nov 1929 flying saucer.jpg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia CommonsFile:Science Wonder Stories Nov 1929 - flying saucer.jpg - Wikimedia Commons...</span></span></span>

### Paperback covers turned spacecraft into everyday symbols

As science fiction moved beyond specialist magazines into affordable paperback editions, cover art brought [UFO imagery]({{ 'ufo-imagery/' | relative_url }}) to a much wider audience. Illustrated covers had to communicate a complete story in a single image, so artists relied on instantly recognisable elements: distant planets, glowing spacecraft, alien landscapes and dramatic encounters between humans and unknown beings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bookriot.com/history-of-sci-fi-book-cover-trends/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bookriot.com">[BOOK RIOT]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bookriot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">BOOK RIOTA History of Sci-Fi Book Cover Trends</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">BOOK RIOTA History of Sci-Fi Book Cover Trends</span></span></span>

The importance of these covers was not only artistic but commercial. A reader browsing a bookshop might encounter dozens of imagined futures through cover paintings before ever seeing a science fiction film. The repeated appearance of similar spacecraft designs gradually made certain forms feel natural: smooth metallic surfaces, domed cockpits, glowing engines and hovering movement became visual shortcuts for [alien technology]({{ 'alien-technology/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bookriot.com/history-of-sci-fi-book-cover-trends/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bookriot.com">[BOOK RIOT]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bookriot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">BOOK RIOTA History of Sci-Fi Book Cover Trends</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">BOOK RIOTA History of Sci-Fi Book Cover Trends</span></span></span>

## Recurring visual formulas

Science fiction artwork created UFO expectations by repeating a small number of powerful visual ideas. These formulas worked because they translated abstract concepts — alien intelligence, technological superiority and unknown destinations — into images that audiences could immediately understand.

**The hovering craft.** Many illustrations presented alien vehicles suspended above Earth rather than travelling like ordinary aircraft. This created a sense that UFOs operated according to unfamiliar technology. The ability to hover, appear silently and ignore normal aviation rules became one of the strongest visual markers of alien presence.

**The glowing encounter.** Artists frequently used bright illumination against dark skies to separate alien technology from ordinary human machines. Later films adopted this approach because light could suggest mystery, scale and otherworldly power without requiring complex explanations.

**The advanced but recognisable machine.** [Early science fiction]({{ 'early-science-fiction/' | relative_url }}) artists often designed spacecraft that looked futuristic while borrowing from familiar engineering principles. They used windows, landing systems, antennae, engines and control areas to make imaginary vehicles appear believable. This balance between the familiar and the impossible became central to later UFO design.

**The human viewpoint.** Many illustrations placed small human figures beneath enormous spacecraft. The comparison of scale communicated vulnerability and wonder, turning the UFO into a symbol of humanity confronting something larger than itself.

These repeated patterns mattered because audiences learned them visually. By the time films began creating their own alien spacecraft, viewers already understood the emotional meaning of a glowing object descending from the sky or a vast machine appearing above a landscape.

## Artwork influencing later films

The transition from illustration to cinema was not a simple replacement of one medium by another. Film-makers often drew directly from the traditions established by science fiction artists, adapting illustrated concepts into moving images.

The 1950s science fiction boom relied heavily on imagery that had already circulated through magazines and books. Films featuring alien machines, distant worlds and mysterious visitors benefited from decades of audience familiarity with illustrated science fiction. The artwork had already established expectations about what alien technology should communicate: power, mystery and a sense of technological advancement.

A later example can be seen in the relationship between concept art and film production. Artists working on major science fiction films often created images that shaped how audiences would eventually see fictional spacecraft on screen. Ralph McQuarrie’s concept paintings for *Star Wars* demonstrate how artwork could establish an entire cinematic universe before filming began, creating visual designs that guided production choices.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.starwars.com/news/celebrating-a-master-inside-star-wars-art-ralph-mcquarrie" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: starwars.com">[StarWars.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">starwars.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Celebrating a Master: Inside Star Wars Art: Ralph McQuarrie &#124; StarWars.comSeptember 20, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 20, 2016</span></span></span>

Although *Star Wars* focused on space adventure rather than UFO encounters specifically, its development shows the continuing role of artwork as a bridge between imagination and cinema. Concept illustrations do not merely represent finished films; they can determine the appearance, atmosphere and emotional tone of the worlds that films create.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.starwars.com/news/celebrating-a-master-inside-star-wars-art-ralph-mcquarrie" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: starwars.com">[StarWars.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">starwars.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Celebrating a Master: Inside Star Wars Art: Ralph McQuarrie &#124; StarWars.comSeptember 20, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 20, 2016</span></span></span>

The same principle shaped UFO imagery in films such as *Close Encounters of the [Third Kind]({{ 'third-kind/' | relative_url }})* (1977), where the spacecraft’s appearance depended on creating a sense of awe rather than simply presenting a machine. The tradition of using light, scale and unusual forms to make [alien craft]({{ 'alien-craft/' | relative_url }}) feel extraordinary had deep roots in earlier science fiction illustration.

## The lasting effect of illustrated UFOs

Science fiction artwork did not establish a single permanent design for UFOs, but it created a collection of expectations that remain influential. The classic flying saucer, the glowing arrival scene and the enormous alien vessel are not purely products of film; they were developed through decades of illustrated speculation.

The lasting significance of this artwork lies in how it shaped imagination before evidence was available. Illustrators gave physical form to ideas that could not be observed directly, allowing readers to picture possible encounters with extraterrestrial technology. Later films inherited these visual conventions because they already carried emotional meaning for audiences.

In this sense, science fiction artwork acted as a testing ground for alien visions. Before cameras, special effects and digital graphics brought UFOs to life on screen, artists had already taught generations how to look at the sky and imagine something unknown arriving from beyond Earth.
