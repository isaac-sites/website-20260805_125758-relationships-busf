## Introduction

Liu Cixin’s *The Three-Body Problem* and its sequels turn [first contact]({{ 'first-contact/' | relative_url }}) into a question of civilisation-level survival rather than simple curiosity or diplomacy. The central conflict between Earth and Trisolaris is built around a harsh possibility: when two intelligent societies meet across interstellar distances, uncertainty itself can become a threat. A civilisation cannot easily know another civilisation’s intentions, future capabilities or willingness to compromise, creating pressure to prepare for the worst.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2019/06/24/liu-cixins-war-of-the-worlds" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: newyorker.com">[The New Yorker]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">newyorker.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The New Yorker Liu Cixin&#x27;s War of the Worlds</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The series mirrors contemporary global tensions, particularly the technological and geopolitical rivalry between China and the U.S., maki...</span></span></span>

## Earth and Trisolaris contact

The first contact between Earth and Trisolaris begins not as a meeting between equals but as a crisis created by desperation. Trisolaris faces an unstable planetary environment caused by its chaotic three-star system, making long-term survival uncertain. Humanity’s discovery of the Trisolarans therefore becomes connected to a migration project: Trisolaris seeks a more stable home, while Earth becomes both a destination and an obstacle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gq.com/story/inside-3-body-problem" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gq.com">[GQ]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gq.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Weiss, creators of Game of Thrones, have embarked on a new project, adapting the Remembrance of Earth&#x27;s Past trilogy by Chinese author Ci...</span></span></span>

A key feature of the conflict is that neither side can rely on normal assumptions about cooperation. Humans initially imagine that communication might lead to understanding, but the power imbalance quickly becomes clear. Trisolaris possesses a significant technological advantage and uses advanced surveillance capabilities to limit Earth’s scientific progress, while humanity attempts to find ways to resist despite being weaker.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2019/06/24/liu-cixins-war-of-the-worlds" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: newyorker.com">[The New Yorker]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">newyorker.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The New Yorker Liu Cixin&#x27;s War of the Worlds</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The series mirrors contemporary global tensions, particularly the technological and geopolitical rivalry between China and the U.S., maki...</span></span></span>

The conflict is also shaped by divisions within Earth itself. The Earth-Trisolaris relationship is not simply humanity versus aliens; human groups disagree about whether alien contact represents salvation, danger or a chance to replace flawed human systems. Ye Wenjie’s decision to reveal Earth’s existence reflects a profound loss of faith in humanity’s ability to govern itself, showing how internal social failures can influence a civilisation’s response to an external threat.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.litcharts.com/lit/the-dark-forest/prologue" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: litcharts.com">[LitCharts]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">litcharts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lit Charts The Dark Forest Prologue</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Dark Forest Prologue Summary &amp; Analysis &#124; LitCharts...</span></span></span>

This makes the series different from many traditional alien invasion stories. The danger does not begin when alien ships arrive. It begins when information about another civilisation enters a world already divided by political conflict, mistrust and competing visions of survival.

## Different survival strategies

### Why hiding becomes a rational choice

The most famous idea developed in the trilogy is the “dark forest” concept, introduced in *The Dark Forest*. It imagines the universe as a place where civilisations remain silent because revealing their location may invite destruction. The logic is based on several assumptions: civilisations prioritise survival, resources are limited, and future intentions cannot be reliably predicted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_forest_hypothesis" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dark forest hypothesis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dark forest hypothesis</span></span></span>

Within this framework, even a peaceful civilisation may appear dangerous because another society cannot know whether its intentions will change over centuries. A technologically weaker civilisation cannot guarantee that a stronger civilisation will remain friendly forever. The safest strategy, in this fictional model, becomes concealment rather than communication.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_forest_hypothesis" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dark forest hypothesis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dark forest hypothesis</span></span></span>

This creates a major tension with the optimistic tradition of [science fiction]({{ 'science-fiction/' | relative_url }}), where contact often leads to knowledge exchange or mutual improvement. *The Three-Body Problem* asks what happens when communication itself carries risk. A message, a signal or a discovery may not be a bridge between worlds; it may reveal a target.

### Technology does not remove vulnerability

The trilogy also challenges the assumption that advanced technology automatically guarantees security. Trisolaris is far ahead of humanity technologically, but it remains vulnerable because it is trapped by the same wider uncertainty about other civilisations. Likewise, humanity’s scientific advances do not provide a simple solution because the main problem is not only engineering — it is strategic decision-making under incomplete information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4336/17/3/22" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mdpi.com">[MDPI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mdpi.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Game Theory Narratives of the Three-Body Trilogy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Game Theory Narratives of the Three-Body TrilogyApril 27, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 27, 2026</span></span></span>

## Civilisation-level conflict

The deepest conflict in *The Three-Body Problem* is not simply humans versus aliens. It is a debate about what a civilisation becomes when survival is its highest priority.

Trisolaris and humanity both make decisions that can appear logical from their own perspective. Trisolaris sees Earth as a possible refuge and therefore as something strategically important. Humanity sees Trisolaris as an existential threat because its arrival could end human independence. The tragedy comes from the fact that both sides can interpret the other’s actions as proof that conflict is unavoidable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2019/06/24/liu-cixins-war-of-the-worlds" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: newyorker.com">[The New Yorker]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">newyorker.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The New Yorker Liu Cixin&#x27;s War of the Worlds</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The series mirrors contemporary global tensions, particularly the technological and geopolitical rivalry between China and the U.S., maki...</span></span></span>

This creates a governance problem on a cosmic scale: who has the authority to make decisions that affect an entire species? The trilogy repeatedly returns to questions about leadership, collective responsibility and whether short-term emotional choices can endanger long-term survival.

Humanity’s attempts to respond also reveal competing survival philosophies. Some characters favour deterrence, secrecy and strategic calculation. Others argue that preserving moral values matters even during existential crises. The conflict is therefore not only about defeating an opponent; it is about deciding what kind of civilisation deserves to survive.

The series’ lasting influence comes from making alien contact a test of civilisation rather than merely a test of technology. It connects UFO and science fiction themes to broader questions about trust, uncertainty and whether intelligent life can overcome fear before fear becomes the reason for conflict. The dark forest idea has since become widely discussed as a fictional interpretation of the Fermi paradox, although it remains a speculative concept rather than an established scientific explanation for the absence of detected extraterrestrial civilisations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/dark-forest-theory-alien-life" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalgeographic.com">[National Geographic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalgeographic.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Geographic The Dark Forest theory explained &#124; National Geographic</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Geographic The Dark Forest theory explained &#124; National Geographic</span></span></span>
