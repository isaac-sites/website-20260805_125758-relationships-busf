## Introduction

H. G. Wells’s *[The War]({{ 'the-war/' | relative_url }}) of the Worlds* (1898) changed alien design by making extraterrestrials not merely strange creatures but intelligent, technologically superior invaders whose appearance reflected humanity’s fear of vulnerability. The Martians were deliberately unlike humans: they were dominated by enormous brains, sensory organs and tentacles rather than familiar limbs and faces. This design choice helped establish a lasting [science-fiction]({{ 'science-fiction/' | relative_url }}) idea that an alien could be both highly intelligent and fundamentally incompatible with human biology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/36/36-h/36-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg The War of the Worlds &#124; Project Gutenberg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg The War of the Worlds &#124; Project Gutenberg</span></span></span>

The importance of Wells’s Martians was not only visual. They transformed the alien from a curiosity into a symbol of invasion, displacement and the possibility that humanity might not be the dominant force on Earth. ## Wells's original alien concept

### A creature built around difference, not resemblance

Wells’s Martians represented a major shift away from earlier ideas of [extraterrestrial life]({{ 'extraterrestrial-life/' | relative_url }}) that often imagined beings as variations of humans or Earth animals. Instead, he created organisms whose bodies suggested a different evolutionary path. In the novel, the Martian is described as having a huge brain, large eyes and groups of delicate tentacles that function as hands. Their bodies are reduced almost entirely to the systems needed for intelligence and survival, making them seem less like monsters and more like a radically altered form of life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/36/36-h/36-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg The War of the Worlds &#124; Project Gutenberg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg The War of the Worlds &#124; Project Gutenberg</span></span></span>

This biological design reinforced the story’s central fear: humans could encounter a civilisation that was not only stronger but also impossible to understand through familiar categories. The Martians were not frightening because they looked like distorted humans; they were frightening because they suggested that intelligence elsewhere in the universe might develop in forms completely unrelated to our own.

The invasion also reversed the usual relationship between explorer and explored. Written during the height of European imperial expansion, the novel placed Britain in the position usually occupied by colonised peoples: a technologically advanced outsider arrives with overwhelming military power and treats the inhabitants as obstacles rather than equals. Many interpretations of the novel connect this reversal to Wells’s criticism of imperial violence and the assumption that technological superiority justified conquest.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sites.psu.edu/historyofthebook/2024/04/17/alien-invasions-vs-imperialism/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sites.psu.edu">[Penn State Sites]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sites.psu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">alien invasions vs imperialism</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Penn State SitesALIEN INVASIONS VS. ### The fear of an unstoppable invader

The Martians’ design worked because their bodies and machines expressed the same idea: human achievement was not enough. Their tripods, heat-rays and scientific abilities made ordinary weapons ineffective, while their alien biology made communication or negotiation almost impossible. The result was a new model for science-fiction invasion stories: the enemy was not simply a foreign army but an entirely different form of civilisation.

This idea became central to later alien portrayals. The alien invader became a way to explore fears about military power, technological imbalance and the possibility that humanity might encounter a force beyond its control.

## Film adaptations and visual changes

### The 1953 Martians become cinematic threats

The [1953 film adaptation]({{ '1953-film/' | relative_url }}) of *The War of the Worlds* transformed Wells’s abstract creatures into one of the most recognisable alien designs in cinema history. Production designer Albert Nozaki created a Martian with a single large eye, long arms and suction-cup fingers, combining biological strangeness with the visual language of 1950s science fiction. The design used the limitations of practical effects to its advantage, creating a creature that appeared mysterious because audiences saw only fragments of its body.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tcm.com/articles/188880/the-war-of-the-worlds-1953-the-war-of-the-worlds-b-1953" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tcm.com">[Turner Classic Movies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tcm.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the war of the worlds 1953 the war of the worlds b 1953</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Turner Classic MoviesThe War of the Worlds (1953) - The War of the Worlds (1953)December 7, 2007...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 7, 2007</span></span></span>

The film also changed the appearance of the Martian war machines. Rather than simply copying the novel’s tripods, the adaptation created floating machines with a distinctive, menacing silhouette and a central eye-like weapon. This connected the alien body and [alien technology]({{ 'alien-technology/' | relative_url }}): both appeared to be extensions of the same hostile intelligence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tcm.com/articles/188880/the-war-of-the-worlds-1953-the-war-of-the-worlds-b-1953" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tcm.com">[Turner Classic Movies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tcm.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the war of the worlds 1953 the war of the worlds b 1953</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Turner Classic MoviesThe War of the Worlds (1953) - The War of the Worlds (1953)December 7, 2007...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 7, 2007</span></span></span>

The changes reflected the concerns of the early Cold War era. The 1953 film presented invasion through the imagery of sudden attack, advanced weapons and civilian helplessness. The Martians became less a Victorian symbol of imperial reversal and more a modern symbol of mass destruction and technological conflict.

### From biological monster to cultural template

The Martians established several features that became common in later alien design: oversized sensory organs, unusual limbs, non-human movement and a connection between appearance and threat. Their influence can be seen in the broader science-fiction tradition of aliens whose bodies communicate their intelligence, danger or unfamiliarity before they speak.

At the same time, later creators often moved away from Wells’s specific design. Some aliens became more humanoid to support character-driven stories, while others became more abstract to emphasise true biological difference. The Martians remain important because they proved that audiences could accept an alien that was intelligent without being recognisably human.

## Invasion themes in alien design

### Why Martians became symbols of human vulnerability

The enduring power of Wells’s Martians comes from the way their design turns physical difference into a larger idea. Their strange bodies represent the possibility that humanity’s assumptions about intelligence, civilisation and dominance may be limited. They are not simply creatures attacking Earth; they are a challenge to the belief that humans occupy a secure position in the universe.

This theme also explains why *The War of the Worlds* became connected to later [UFO culture]({{ 'ufo-culture/' | relative_url }}). Although UFO reports and science fiction developed through different channels, fictional aliens provided a vocabulary for imagining unknown visitors. The Martians helped create one of the earliest and most influential images of an extraterrestrial intelligence arriving with motives that humans could not easily predict.

### The 1938 broadcast and the power of invasion imagery

The [Martian invasion]({{ 'martian-invasion/' | relative_url }}) became especially powerful through Orson Welles’s 1938 radio adaptation, which presented the story as a series of realistic news reports. Some listeners believed the fictional broadcast described a genuine emergency, particularly those who joined after the introduction explaining it was a drama. Contemporary reports described confusion and fear, although later historians have argued that the scale of the panic was exaggerated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/war-of-the-worlds-behind-the-panic" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalgeographic.com">[National Geographic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalgeographic.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">war of the worlds behind the panic</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Geographic&#x27;War of the Worlds&#x27;: Behind the 1938 Radio Show Panic &#124; National GeographicJune 17, 2005...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 17, 2005</span></span></span>

Regardless of the exact size of the reaction, the event demonstrated the strength of the invasion concept created by Wells. A fictional attack from Mars was convincing enough to interact with real-world fears about war, technology and uncertainty. The Martians had moved beyond being literary characters: they had become a cultural image of sudden extraterrestrial threat.

## Why the Martians changed alien design forever

The lasting influence of *The War of the Worlds* lies in its combination of biology and fear. Wells did not create aliens that were simply humans with unusual features; he created beings whose bodies suggested a separate evolutionary history and whose arrival exposed human weakness. Later science fiction repeatedly adapted this approach, using alien design to explore anxieties about invasion, identity and humanity’s place in the cosmos.

The Martians therefore occupy a foundational position in the relationship between UFO ideas and science fiction. They helped establish the expectation that an alien encounter would not just involve discovering another species, but confronting an intelligence that might challenge human power, knowledge and survival.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/mississippi-scholarship-online/book/51370/chapter-abstract/417739682" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicThe War of the Worlds and Alien Making &#124; Visions of Invasion: Alien Affects, Cinema, and Citizenship in Settler Colonies &#124; Mi...</span></span></span>

    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
