## Introduction

“Close Encounter of the Third Kind” became one of the most recognisable phrases in [UFO culture]({{ 'ufo-culture/' | relative_url }}) because it transformed a technical classification into a shared story idea: the moment when humans and imagined alien beings meet face to face. Created by astronomer J. Allen Hynek as part of his attempt to classify UFO reports, the term originally described alleged sightings involving occupants of a craft. Its lasting influence came less from the classification itself than from how [science fiction]({{ 'science-fiction/' | relative_url }}) adopted it as a symbol of communication, curiosity and the possibility of peaceful contact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tcm.com/articles/467312/the-big-idea-close-encounters-of-the-third-kind" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tcm.com">[Turner Classic Movies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tcm.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Turner Classic Movies The Big Idea</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Turner Classic MoviesThe Big Idea - Close Encounters of the Third KindDecember 30, 2011...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 30, 2011</span></span></span>

The phrase reached a global audience through Steven Spielberg’s 1977 film *Close Encounters of the Third Kind*, which turned [Hynek]({{ 'hynek/' | relative_url }})’s terminology into a cultural shorthand for alien contact narratives. Rather than presenting extraterrestrials mainly as invaders, the film imagined [first contact]({{ 'first-contact/' | relative_url }}) as a process of discovery, emotional transformation and attempted understanding. That shift helped redefine how popular science fiction portrayed encounters between humanity and unknown intelligences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.loc.gov/item/event-421188/close-encounters-of-the-third-kind-1977/2026-06-05/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Library of CongressClose Encounters of the Third Kind (1977)* &#124; Library of CongressJune 5, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 5, 2026</span></span></span>

## Why Third Kind Encounters Became Iconic

The cultural power of the third kind category came from the idea that seeing an alien occupant represented a different kind of relationship with the unknown. Earlier science fiction often focused on threats from space: invasions, conquest or humanity’s struggle against superior technology. The third kind concept introduced a more intimate scenario — not simply observing a mysterious object, but encountering another form of life.

Hynek’s category provided writers and filmmakers with a simple dramatic boundary. A strange light in the sky could remain a mystery, and a physical effect could suggest an unexplained event, but the appearance of an occupant created a story about communication between species. The category therefore became useful as a narrative device because it gave science fiction a clear moment of transition: humanity moved from wondering whether something was out there to asking what could happen if it answered back.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tcm.com/articles/467312/the-big-idea-close-encounters-of-the-third-kind" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tcm.com">[Turner Classic Movies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tcm.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Turner Classic Movies The Big Idea</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Turner Classic MoviesThe Big Idea - Close Encounters of the Third KindDecember 30, 2011...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 30, 2011</span></span></span>

This idea was especially influential during a period when science fiction was expanding beyond traditional space adventure. The late 1960s and 1970s saw more stories using [extraterrestrial life]({{ 'extraterrestrial-life/' | relative_url }}) to explore [human identity]({{ 'human-identity/' | relative_url }}), social fears and hopes about the future. The third kind encounter fitted this change because it framed aliens as a possible mirror for humanity rather than simply an enemy.

### The meaning of alien occupants

In UFO reporting, “occupants” referred to claimed beings associated with a UFO sighting. In science fiction, however, the idea became much broader. Alien figures could represent unfamiliar cultures, alternative forms of intelligence or questions about humanity’s place in the universe.

*Close Encounters of the Third Kind* used this possibility at the centre of its story. The aliens are mysterious, but they are not portrayed as a military force seeking domination. The film’s tension comes from uncertainty: whether humans can overcome fear and interpret the arrival as an opportunity for contact. The Library of Congress describes the film as focusing on ordinary people affected by UFO encounters while scientists prepare for contact, highlighting the film’s emphasis on interaction rather than simple conflict.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.loc.gov/item/event-421188/close-encounters-of-the-third-kind-1977/2026-06-05/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Library of CongressClose Encounters of the Third Kind (1977)* &#124; Library of CongressJune 5, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 5, 2026</span></span></span>

This approach influenced later science fiction by making alien communication a major storytelling theme. The question changed from “How do we defeat an alien civilisation?” to “How would two different intelligences understand each other?” That framework appears in many later works that explore language, diplomacy and the challenges of recognising intelligence unlike our own.

## Cinema’s Use of Hynek’s Term

The 1977 film was the decisive moment that moved Hynek’s vocabulary from specialist UFO discussions into mainstream culture. Spielberg used the phrase not merely as a title but as a promise of a particular kind of story: an encounter where the unknown becomes personal. Hynek himself served as a scientific consultant for the production, linking the fictional film to the classification system that inspired its name.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Close_Encounters_of_the_Third_Kind" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Close Encounters of the Third Kind</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Close Encounters of the Third Kind</span></span></span>

The film’s influence came from combining scientific language with emotional storytelling. By borrowing a phrase that sounded like a formal category, Hollywood gave an imagined event the feeling of something being carefully studied and documented. At the same time, Spielberg used the concept to create a human drama centred on obsession, wonder and the desire to understand a larger universe.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.loc.gov/item/event-421188/close-encounters-of-the-third-kind-1977/2026-06-05/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Library of CongressClose Encounters of the Third Kind (1977)* &#124; Library of CongressJune 5, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 5, 2026</span></span></span>

The film’s success also helped preserve the phrase in everyday culture. References, parodies and variations of “close encounters” appeared across popular media because audiences immediately understood the idea behind the wording: an unusual meeting with something beyond ordinary human experience. Turner Classic Movies notes that the film popularised the term so widely that later uses often referenced the movie’s cultural meaning rather than Hynek’s original classification system.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tcm.com/articles/467315/pop-culture-101-close-encounters-of-the-third-kind" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tcm.com">[Turner Classic Movies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tcm.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Turner Classic Movies Pop Culture 101</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Turner Classic MoviesPop Culture 101 - Close Encounters of the Third KindDecember 30, 2011...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 30, 2011</span></span></span>

The film’s legacy was recognised beyond popular entertainment. The Library of Congress selected *Close Encounters of the Third Kind* for preservation in the National Film Registry, identifying it as culturally, historically or aesthetically significant.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.loc.gov/programs/national-film-preservation-board/film-registry/complete-national-film-registry-listing/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Library of CongressComplete National Film Registry Listing &#124; Film Registry &#124; National Film Preservation Board &#124; Programs &#124; Library of...</span></span></span> This status reflects the film’s importance not only as a science fiction work but as a major influence on how modern culture imagines contact with extraterrestrial life.

## Contact Instead of Conflict Narratives

One of the most important changes associated with the third kind idea was the move from alien invasion stories towards contact stories. This did not replace hostile alien narratives, but it created a powerful alternative model in which the arrival of extraterrestrial beings could represent a challenge of understanding rather than a battle for survival.

*Close Encounters of the Third Kind* became a defining example because its climax is built around communication. The famous musical exchange between humans and the visiting beings presents contact as a shared language rather than a weapon. The aliens’ arrival becomes a test of humanity’s ability to respond with curiosity and cooperation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bfi.org.uk/film/d0bb1ad2-c6a4-551e-a53d-67f50be618f9/close-encounters-of-the-third-kind" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bfi.org.uk">[BFI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bfi.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">close encounters of the third kind</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Close Encounters of the Third Kind (1977) &#124; BFI...</span></span></span>

This theme became one of the foundations of later optimistic science fiction. Stories about alien communication increasingly explored translation, diplomacy and cultural difference. The third kind encounter became a fictional model for humanity meeting an unfamiliar civilisation and asking whether intelligence elsewhere in the universe might be approached through dialogue.

The legacy is also significant because the concept remained connected to real-world UFO culture while becoming independent from it. Hynek’s original classification was designed to organise reports, not to prove extraterrestrial visitation. Popular culture transformed the third kind category into a symbol of imagined first contact — a creative idea that could be used by filmmakers and writers regardless of whether individual UFO claims were accepted as evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tcm.com/articles/467312/the-big-idea-close-encounters-of-the-third-kind" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tcm.com">[Turner Classic Movies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tcm.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Turner Classic Movies The Big Idea</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Turner Classic MoviesThe Big Idea - Close Encounters of the Third KindDecember 30, 2011...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 30, 2011</span></span></span>

The enduring appeal of the third kind encounter lies in that tension. It began as a classification of reported experiences, but it survived because it expressed a much larger human question: if another intelligence exists beyond Earth, would humanity respond with fear, conflict or a desire to communicate? In science fiction, the third kind became the narrative space where that question could be explored.
