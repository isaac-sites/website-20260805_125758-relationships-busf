## Introduction

Early fictional rockets looked like the machines of the industrial age because writers had no real spacecraft to copy. Before rockets became practical vehicles, authors imagined space travel by extending technologies they already understood: naval vessels, railway locomotives, artillery shells and aircraft. These imagined vehicles were futuristic, but their shapes and operating ideas were often built from familiar engineering assumptions about engines, metal structures, enclosed cabins and mechanical power. This connection explains why [early science fiction]({{ 'early-science-fiction/' | relative_url }}) spacecraft often resembled advanced versions of earthly machines rather than the streamlined rockets and capsules that later defined the Space Age.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://airandspace.si.edu/explore/stories/science-fiction" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airandspace.si.edu">[Smithsonian Air and Space Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airandspace.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Smithsonian Air and Space Museum Science Fiction &#124; National Air and Space Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Smithsonian Air and Space Museum Science Fiction &#124; National Air and Space Museum</span></span></span>

## Industrial influences on spacecraft

### Why early rockets resembled ships and machines

Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century technology shaped how authors imagined leaving Earth. The dominant machines of the industrial era were large, mechanical and highly visible: steamships crossed oceans, railways connected continents, factories produced complex metal structures, and artillery demonstrated the power of controlled explosions. When writers imagined a journey beyond Earth, they naturally borrowed from these examples.

A fictional spacecraft therefore often functioned like a combination of a ship and a vehicle of exploration. It had a hull to protect passengers, an internal space for a crew, instruments for navigation and a powerful propulsion system. The idea of travelling through space was treated as an extension of existing exploration rather than a completely new form of movement. The spacecraft was frequently imagined as a vessel that carried humans through a hostile environment, much like a submarine beneath the sea or a ship crossing an unknown ocean.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://airandspace.si.edu/explore/stories/science-fiction" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airandspace.si.edu">[Smithsonian Air and Space Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airandspace.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Smithsonian Air and Space Museum Science Fiction &#124; National Air and Space Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Smithsonian Air and Space Museum Science Fiction &#124; National Air and Space Museum</span></span></span>

This approach also affected the scale of fictional rockets. Early illustrations and stories often presented enormous vehicles because industrial culture associated size with power. A larger machine suggested stronger engines, greater carrying capacity and greater national or scientific ambition. These designs reflected the age of steamships and heavy engineering, where technological progress was commonly represented through increasingly massive structures.

### Artillery, trains and the idea of launching into space

One of the clearest examples of industrial thinking in early space fiction appears in Jules Verne’s *From the Earth to the Moon* (1865). Verne did not imagine a modern rocket launch; instead, his travellers were sent toward the Moon inside a projectile fired from the giant Columbiad cannon built by the Baltimore Gun Club. The method reflected the nineteenth century’s confidence in artillery, metallurgy and precision engineering. The spacecraft was essentially a highly advanced cannon projectile adapted for human passengers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.space.com/entertainment/how-jules-verne-predicted-the-artemis-2-mission-to-the-moon-almost-160-years-ago" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: space.com">[Space]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">space.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">How Jules Verne predicted the Artemis 2 mission to the moon almost 160 years ago</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Published in 1865, Verne’s story involves a projectile launched to the moon from Florida, near the equator—mirroring NASA’s launch site a...</span></span></span>

Although the mechanism was scientifically inaccurate by modern standards, the concept revealed an important imaginative step. Verne was taking a familiar industrial technology — the cannon — and pushing it into an extreme future application. The question was not yet “what kind of vehicle naturally belongs in space?” but rather “how can the most powerful machine of the industrial world be enlarged until it reaches space?”<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.space.com/entertainment/how-jules-verne-predicted-the-artemis-2-mission-to-the-moon-almost-160-years-ago" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: space.com">[Space]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">space.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">How Jules Verne predicted the Artemis 2 mission to the moon almost 160 years ago</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Published in 1865, Verne’s story involves a projectile launched to the moon from Florida, near the equator—mirroring NASA’s launch site a...</span></span></span>

This pattern appeared repeatedly in early [science fiction]({{ 'science-fiction/' | relative_url }}). Authors often imagined progress as an accumulation of existing technologies: stronger engines, tougher metals and larger machines. Space travel became the next stage of industrial expansion.

## Verne and Wells approaches

### Verne’s engineered projectile

Verne’s approach was closely tied to nineteenth-century engineering culture. His fictional spacecraft was a carefully described technical object, with attention given to construction, launch conditions and the practical challenges faced by travellers. The limitations of the design reveal the period’s technological assumptions: the spacecraft had to be launched by a known force, and the solution came from improving existing machinery rather than inventing an entirely new propulsion system.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.space.com/entertainment/how-jules-verne-predicted-the-artemis-2-mission-to-the-moon-almost-160-years-ago" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: space.com">[Space]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">space.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">How Jules Verne predicted the Artemis 2 mission to the moon almost 160 years ago</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Published in 1865, Verne’s story involves a projectile launched to the moon from Florida, near the equator—mirroring NASA’s launch site a...</span></span></span>

The importance of Verne’s design was not that it predicted a real rocket. Instead, it showed how industrial imagination could transform familiar mechanisms into visions of the future. The cannon became a gateway to space, just as railways and steamships had opened new regions on Earth.

### Wells and the alien machine beyond human industry

H. G. Wells approached spacecraft differently in *[The War]({{ 'the-war/' | relative_url }}) of the Worlds* (1898). His Martian vehicle was not a human-built rocket but an alien projectile: a cylindrical craft fired from Mars and arriving on Earth. The machine still carried industrial characteristics, however. It was a constructed object made of metal, designed for transport and capable of surviving a difficult journey between worlds.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://etc.usf.edu/lit2go/135/the-war-of-the-worlds/2461/book-onethe-coming-of-the-martians-chapter-4-the-cylinder-opens/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: etc.usf.edu">[ETC at USF]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">etc.usf.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on usf.edu.</span></span></span>

The difference between Verne and Wells is significant. Verne imagined humans extending their own engineering abilities into space, while Wells imagined a civilisation whose technology had advanced beyond humanity’s. Yet both writers used the industrial language of machines, materials and transport systems. Their spacecraft were not magical objects; they were technological devices with a physical presence.

This distinction later influenced science fiction’s relationship with [UFO imagery]({{ 'ufo-imagery/' | relative_url }}). Some fictional spacecraft represented human progress, while others represented unknown visitors arriving in superior machines. Both traditions depended on the same early idea: that advanced beings would travel in engineered vehicles.

## Changing ideas of space vehicles

### From industrial vessels to dedicated rockets

The image of the spacecraft began changing as rocket science developed in the early twentieth century. Real rocket pioneers and theoretical studies introduced the possibility that rockets could operate through their own propulsion rather than being fired like artillery shells. The National Air and Space Museum notes that twentieth-century advances in rocket propulsion transformed rockets from earlier devices into technologies capable of changing space exploration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://airandspace.si.edu/explore/topics/technology-and-engineering/rockets" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airandspace.si.edu">[Smithsonian Air and Space Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airandspace.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Smithsonian Air and Space Museum Rockets &#124; National Air and Space Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Smithsonian Air and Space Museum Rockets &#124; National Air and Space Museum</span></span></span>

Science fiction magazines helped spread this new vision. When Hugo Gernsback launched *[Amazing Stories]({{ 'amazing-stories/' | relative_url }})* in 1926, he promoted stories that combined scientific ideas with imagination. The magazine featured earlier writers such as Verne and Wells alongside new space adventures, while artists created dramatic images of rockets and other future machines.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bis-space.com/from-amazing-stories-to-the-bis-100-years-of-scientifiction/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bis-space.com">[The British Interplanetary Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bis-space.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The British Interplanetary SocietyFrom Amazing Stories to the BIS: 100 Years of “Scientifiction” - The British Interplanetary SocietyMarc...</span></span></span>

These illustrations gradually moved spacecraft away from the appearance of ordinary industrial vehicles. Rockets became taller, more streamlined and more clearly designed for flight beyond Earth. However, many still retained familiar features: windows like ships, metal bodies like industrial machines and engines resembling exaggerated versions of aircraft technology.

### The industrial legacy in later spacecraft imagination

Even when fictional spacecraft became more advanced, the industrial foundations remained visible. Early science fiction established several lasting assumptions: spacecraft would be mechanical, constructed, powerful and purposeful. They would have interiors, controls and systems that reflected human ideas of engineering.

This legacy helped shape the wider cultural image of unknown spacecraft. When [UFO reports]({{ 'ufo-reports/' | relative_url }}) emerged in the twentieth century, many people interpreted unusual objects through a visual language already developed by science fiction. The connection was not that fictional rockets predicted UFOs, but that both drew from the same human habit of imagining advanced vehicles through the technologies of their own time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bis-space.com/from-amazing-stories-to-the-bis-100-years-of-scientifiction/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bis-space.com">[The British Interplanetary Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bis-space.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The British Interplanetary SocietyFrom Amazing Stories to the BIS: 100 Years of “Scientifiction” - The British Interplanetary SocietyMarc...</span></span></span>

Early fictional rockets therefore occupy an important middle ground between the industrial world and the Space Age. They were not accurate models of future spacecraft, but they provided a bridge between familiar machines and the possibility of travel beyond Earth.
