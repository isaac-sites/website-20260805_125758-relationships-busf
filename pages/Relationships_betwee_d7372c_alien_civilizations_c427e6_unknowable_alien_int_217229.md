## Introduction

[Science fiction]({{ 'science-fiction/' | relative_url }})’s most challenging aliens are often not the ones with the strangest bodies, but the ones whose minds resist human interpretation. The idea of an unknowable alien intelligence asks a deeper question than “what would extraterrestrials look like?” It asks whether humans could even recognise a form of thought that evolved under completely different conditions. Stories built around this possibility suggest that [first contact]({{ 'first-contact/' | relative_url }}) might not be a simple conversation between two species, but a confrontation with the limits of human perception, language and imagination.

This theme connects directly to debates around UFOs and science fiction because fictional alien minds have shaped expectations about what extraterrestrial contact might mean. Rather than presenting aliens as humans with different appearances, works such as Stanisław Lem’s *[Solaris]({{ 'solaris/' | relative_url }})*, Ted Chiang’s *Story of Your Life* and its adaptation *Arrival*, and later explorations of non-human intelligence challenge the assumption that intelligence must resemble our own. They examine a possibility that also concerns real SETI researchers: an advanced civilisation may exist, but our methods of recognising or communicating with it may be limited by human assumptions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://communicating.seti.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: communicating.seti.org">[communicating.seti.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">communicating.seti.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Welcome &#124; Communicating Across the Cosmos</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Welcome &#124; Communicating Across the Cosmos</span></span></span>

## Beyond humanoid alien designs

Many [science fiction aliens]({{ 'science-fiction-alien/' | relative_url }}) remain understandable because they are built around familiar human qualities. They may have different bodies or cultures, but they still communicate through language, pursue goals, form societies and experience emotions in ways audiences can recognise. The more radical tradition of alien fiction removes those human reference points and asks what intelligence might look like if it developed through a completely different evolutionary path.

Stanisław Lem’s 1961 novel *Solaris* is one of the most influential examples. Instead of imagining aliens as creatures from another planet, Lem presents an entire planetary ocean that may itself be a vast intelligence. Human scientists studying Solaris spend decades attempting to classify its behaviour, but they cannot determine whether its actions represent communication, experimentation, indifference or something outside human categories altogether. The novel’s central tension is not whether humans can defeat or befriend the alien, but whether they possess the conceptual tools needed to understand it. Literary analysis of Lem’s work often highlights this idea of an “alien” alien: a mind so different that ordinary scientific approaches struggle to describe it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://english.lem.pl/works/novels/solaris/2-introduction-solaris" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: english.lem.pl">[Stanislaw Lem The Official Site]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">english.lem.pl</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Stanislaw Lem The Official Site Stanisław Lem</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Stanislaw Lem The Official SiteStanisław Lem - Solaris...</span></span></span>

The importance of *Solaris* is that it rejects a common science-fiction shortcut: making aliens understandable by giving them human motivations. The Solaris ocean does not clearly seek conquest, cooperation or survival in a familiar sense. Its behaviour forces researchers to question whether human ideas such as intention, consciousness and communication are universal or merely products of Earth’s own evolutionary history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://english.lem.pl/works/novels/solaris/2-introduction-solaris" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: english.lem.pl">[Stanislaw Lem The Official Site]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">english.lem.pl</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Stanislaw Lem The Official Site Stanisław Lem</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Stanislaw Lem The Official SiteStanisław Lem - Solaris...</span></span></span>

A similar challenge appears in more recent fiction involving artificial intelligence, collective minds and non-biological forms of cognition. These stories extend the alien question beyond biology: even if an intelligence is not hostile or primitive, humans may struggle to interpret it because its priorities, time scale or methods of reasoning operate differently.

## Different forms of perception

The difficulty of understanding alien intelligence is often presented through perception. Humans experience reality through a limited set of senses and cognitive structures shaped by life on Earth. Science fiction uses alien minds to imagine what happens when intelligence develops with a different relationship to space, time, language or physical existence.

*Arrival* provides one of the clearest examples through its depiction of the heptapods, whose written language is organised in circular forms rather than human linear sequences. The story, adapted from Ted Chiang’s 1998 novella *Story of Your Life*, focuses on the problem of communication rather than invasion. Linguist Louise Banks must first understand how the aliens organise meaning before attempting to interpret their intentions. The film’s scientific consultants included linguists who helped ground its exploration of language learning and communication, while scholars have used the story to discuss questions about linguistic structure and how language may influence thought.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/story/arrival-science-fact-fiction/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arrival science fact fiction</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The science of Arrival: what the film got right (and wrong) &#124; WIREDNovember 12, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 12, 2016</span></span></span>

The underlying idea is not that learning an [alien language]({{ 'alien-language/' | relative_url }}) would magically transform human consciousness, as the fictional story imagines, but that language can reveal how differently minds may organise experience. Human languages already differ in how they describe concepts such as time, direction and relationships. An extraterrestrial communication system could be shaped by an entirely different biology and environment, making translation far more difficult than replacing words from one language with another.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/book/31973/chapter-abstract/267717021" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicThe linguistics of Arrival: Heptapods, field linguistics, and Universal Grammar &#124; Language Invention in Linguistics Pedagogy...</span></span></span>

This issue is also important in real discussions of communication with extraterrestrial intelligence. SETI researchers have considered how humanity might construct a message that could be understood by a civilisation with no shared culture, history or biology. The challenge is not only sending a signal across space, but deciding which assumptions about intelligence and meaning are safe to make.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://communicating.seti.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: communicating.seti.org">[communicating.seti.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">communicating.seti.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Welcome &#124; Communicating Across the Cosmos</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Welcome &#124; Communicating Across the Cosmos</span></span></span>

Science fiction turns this communication problem into a narrative experiment. If humans cannot easily understand another human culture without shared experiences, the gap with an alien civilisation could be far greater. An extraterrestrial mind might not use symbols in ways humans recognise, value information in familiar forms or even divide reality into concepts similar to ours.

## Science fiction and cosmic uncertainty

Unknowable aliens change the purpose of the UFO and first-contact story. Earlier science fiction often used extraterrestrials to represent a clear external challenge: invasion, competition or technological superiority. The unknowable alien instead creates uncertainty. The encounter becomes a test of human humility rather than human strength.

This approach has influenced how science fiction frames the search for alien life. Real programmes searching for extraterrestrial intelligence often begin with assumptions based on known physics and technology, such as looking for detectable signals produced by advanced societies. Researchers recognise that communication with a civilisation unlike humanity raises difficult questions about what counts as a meaningful signal and whether human expectations could limit what we are able to detect.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://communicating.seti.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: communicating.seti.org">[communicating.seti.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">communicating.seti.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Welcome &#124; Communicating Across the Cosmos</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Welcome &#124; Communicating Across the Cosmos</span></span></span>

However, the idea of an unknowable alien should not be misunderstood as proof that alien intelligence would be impossible to understand. Science itself depends on discovering patterns in unfamiliar phenomena. Humans have learned to interpret many forms of non-human behaviour, from animal communication to complex natural systems. The more careful lesson from science fiction is that understanding may require abandoning assumptions rather than simply collecting more information.

The strongest alien stories therefore do not argue that contact is impossible. They explore the possibility that contact would require intellectual adaptation. Humanity might encounter intelligence that does not fit existing categories of organism, society, communication or consciousness. In that scenario, the greatest discovery would not only be another civilisation, but a clearer understanding of the boundaries of the human mind.

By portraying aliens that cannot be easily translated into human terms, science fiction turns UFO-related imagination into a philosophical question: not just whether we are alone in the universe, but whether we are prepared to recognise something genuinely different when we find it.
