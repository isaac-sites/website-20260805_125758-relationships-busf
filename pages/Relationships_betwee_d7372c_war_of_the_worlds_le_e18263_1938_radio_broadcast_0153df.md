## Introduction

The 1938 radio adaptation of *[The War]({{ 'the-war/' | relative_url }}) of the Worlds* became famous as the broadcast that supposedly convinced Americans that Martians had invaded Earth. The reality is more complicated: some listeners did mistake the programme for a genuine emergency, but later research suggests that the scale of the “mass panic” was greatly exaggerated. The event’s lasting importance lies less in a nationwide collapse into fear and more in the way a fictional performance demonstrated radio’s ability to make imagined extraterrestrial threats feel immediate and real.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_War_of_the_Worlds_%281938_radio_drama%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The War of the Worlds (1938 radio drama</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The War of the Worlds (1938 radio drama</span></span></span>

Broadcast on 30 October 1938 by Orson Welles’s *Mercury Theatre on the Air*, the adaptation transformed H. G. Wells’s alien invasion story into a simulated news interruption. By using the language and sound of live reporting, it created a powerful example of fiction crossing into the territory of public belief. This made the programme a defining moment in the relationship between [UFO culture]({{ 'ufo-culture/' | relative_url }}) and [science fiction]({{ 'science-fiction/' | relative_url }}): it showed how stories about visitors from space could influence how audiences imagined real possibilities of extraterrestrial contact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wellesnet.com/the-war-of-the-worlds-radio-script/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wellesnet.com">[Wellesnet &#124; The Orson Welles Resource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wellesnet.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the war of the worlds radio script</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Orson Welles Resource&#x27;The War of the Worlds&#x27; radio script from October 30, 1938October 9, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 30, 1938</span></span></span>

## The simulated news format

### How the broadcast created realism

The programme’s unusual structure was central to its effect. Instead of presenting the story as a conventional radio drama with a clear sequence of scenes, the adaptation interrupted a fictional music programme with urgent “news bulletins” describing strange events in New Jersey. Reporters, officials and witnesses appeared to provide live updates as the imaginary invasion developed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wellesnet.com/the-war-of-the-worlds-radio-script/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wellesnet.com">[Wellesnet &#124; The Orson Welles Resource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wellesnet.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the war of the worlds radio script</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Orson Welles Resource&#x27;The War of the Worlds&#x27; radio script from October 30, 1938October 9, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 30, 1938</span></span></span>

The production relied on techniques that audiences already associated with serious radio journalism. In the late 1930s, radio had become a trusted source for breaking information, especially during moments of crisis. The realistic presentation style borrowed the authority of news broadcasting while applying it to science fiction. The result was not simply a story about aliens; it was a performance designed to feel like an unfolding event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/book/3693/chapter-abstract/145077697" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicRadio and the Intelligent Listener: The War of the Worlds Panic &#124; Radio&#x27;s Civic Ambition: American Broadcasting and Democracy...</span></span></span>

One important factor was that some listeners joined the programme after it had already begun. The introduction identified the broadcast as a dramatic adaptation, but people who tuned in later could hear only the fictional emergency reports. The popularity of the rival *Chase and Sanborn Hour* also contributed to the confusion because some listeners switched channels during that programme and missed the opening explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/war-of-the-worlds-behind-the-panic" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalgeographic.com">[National Geographic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalgeographic.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">war of the worlds behind the panic</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Geographic&#x27;War of the Worlds&#x27;: Behind the 1938 Radio Show Panic &#124; National Geographic...</span></span></span>

The broadcast therefore exploited a specific historical moment: radio was both entertainment and a trusted public information system. The audience was not merely reacting to a frightening story; some were responding to a format that resembled the emergency communications they expected during a real crisis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/book/3693/chapter-abstract/145077697" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicRadio and the Intelligent Listener: The War of the Worlds Panic &#124; Radio&#x27;s Civic Ambition: American Broadcasting and Democracy...</span></span></span>

## Claims of public panic examined

### What people believed happened

Contemporary newspaper reports described dramatic scenes: frightened listeners calling police stations, leaving their homes, or preparing to escape the supposed invasion. These accounts created the enduring image of a country gripped by alien panic. A widely repeated version of the story suggested that millions of people accepted the [Martian invasion]({{ 'martian-invasion/' | relative_url }}) as fact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://paw.princeton.edu/article/professor-hadley-cantril-studied-hysteria-after-war-worlds" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: princeton.edu">[Princeton Alumni Weekly]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">princeton.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Alumni Weekly Professor Hadley Cantril Studied Hysteria After ‘War of the Worlds’ &#124; Princeton Alumni Weekly</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Princeton Alumni WeeklyProfessor Hadley Cantril Studied Hysteria After ‘War of the Worlds’ &#124; Princeton Alumni WeeklySeptember 24, 2021</span></span></span>

The first major academic study of the reaction, psychologist Hadley Cantril’s *The Invasion from Mars* (1940), estimated that around six million people heard the programme, that 1.7 million believed it was a news report, and that 1.2 million experienced fear or disturbance. Cantril’s work became the foundation for the traditional account of a major public panic.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_War_of_the_Worlds_%281938_radio_drama%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The War of the Worlds (1938 radio drama</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The War of the Worlds (1938 radio drama</span></span></span>

However, later historians have challenged the scale and interpretation of those findings. Researchers Michael Socolow and A. Brad Schwartz have argued that the evidence does not support the idea of a nationwide outbreak of mass hysteria. Schwartz’s examination of listener responses and contemporary records found that many reactions involved confusion, curiosity, anger or concern rather than a belief that Earth was actually being invaded.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://abradschwartz.com/broadcast-hysteria/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abradschwartz.com">[A. ### Why the panic story became so powerful

The legend of the broadcast panic was shaped partly by the reaction that followed the programme. Newspapers highlighted examples of frightened listeners and criticised radio’s growing influence, helping turn the event into a wider debate about whether broadcasters had too much power. The controversy was not only about aliens; it was about trust, technology and the responsibility of mass media.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2017/09/04/the-fake-news-fallacy" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: newyorker.com">[The New Yorker]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">newyorker.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">While the event is remembered for creating mass hysteria, researcher A. Some historians argue that newspapers had reasons to emphasise the idea of widespread panic because radio was becoming a major competitor for public attention. By portraying radio audiences as easily misled, critics could question the reliability of the newer medium. The debate reflected broader 1930s concerns about propaganda, persuasion and whether citizens could critically evaluate powerful forms of communication.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/book/3693/chapter-abstract/145077697" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicRadio and the Intelligent Listener: The War of the Worlds Panic &#124; Radio&#x27;s Civic Ambition: American Broadcasting and Democracy...</span></span></span>

This does not mean nobody was frightened. Some listeners genuinely misunderstood the programme, and the realistic presentation produced real moments of anxiety. The historical correction is that isolated cases of confusion should not automatically be treated as proof of a national psychological collapse.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://abradschwartz.com/broadcast-hysteria/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abradschwartz.com">[A. ## Legacy for UFO culture

The importance of the 1938 broadcast extends beyond the question of how many people panicked. It became a cultural example of how fictional alien scenarios could shape expectations about extraterrestrial encounters. By making a Martian invasion sound like a current event, the programme demonstrated that science fiction could influence the emotional and interpretive frameworks people used when thinking about unknown phenomena.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/book/3693/chapter-abstract/145077697" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicRadio and the Intelligent Listener: The War of the Worlds Panic &#124; Radio&#x27;s Civic Ambition: American Broadcasting and Democracy...</span></span></span>

This became especially significant as UFO culture developed after the Second World War. Reports of unexplained objects in the sky were often interpreted through ideas already familiar from science fiction: advanced visitors from other worlds, mysterious technology and possible threats from beyond Earth. The 1938 broadcast did not create belief in UFOs, but it showed how strongly fictional representations of aliens could affect public imagination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.princeton.edu/news/2018/10/15/history-phd-student-schwartz-war-worlds-still-resonates-80-years-later" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: princeton.edu">[Princeton University]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">princeton.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Princeton UniversityHistory Ph.D. student Schwartz: ‘War of the Worlds’ still resonates 80 years laterOctober 15, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 15, 2018</span></span></span>

The broadcast also remains a key case study in the boundary between fiction and reality. Its lesson is not simply that audiences can be fooled. Instead, it reveals a more complex relationship between storytelling, technology and belief: realistic fiction can borrow the authority of real-world communication systems, while audiences interpret unfamiliar possibilities through stories they already know.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://abradschwartz.com/broadcast-hysteria/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abradschwartz.com">[A. For UFO culture and science fiction, the 1938 *War of the Worlds* adaptation became a landmark because it showed the social power of an imagined alien encounter. The Martians never arrived, but the broadcast proved that a carefully constructed fictional arrival from space could produce a very real conversation about fear, trust and humanity’s place in the universe.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.princeton.edu/news/2018/10/15/history-phd-student-schwartz-war-worlds-still-resonates-80-years-later" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: princeton.edu">[Princeton University]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">princeton.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Princeton UniversityHistory Ph.D. student Schwartz: ‘War of the Worlds’ still resonates 80 years laterOctober 15, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 15, 2018</span></span></span>
