## Introduction

The debate over sending messages to aliens is one of the clearest real-world examples of the tension between peaceful contact and [invasion fears]({{ 'invasion-fears/' | relative_url }}) in the UFO and [science-fiction]({{ 'science-fiction/' | relative_url }}) tradition. Known as Messaging Extraterrestrial Intelligence (METI), the practice involves deliberately transmitting signals into space in the hope that an extraterrestrial civilisation might detect and understand them. Supporters argue that humanity should not only listen for other civilisations but also introduce itself, while critics warn that announcing Earth’s location could create unknown risks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://iaaseti.org/en/san-marino-scale/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: iaaseti.org">[IAASeti]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">iaaseti.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">San Marino Scale</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">San Marino Scale...</span></span></span>

## Why scientists consider active messaging

### From listening to speaking

Traditional SETI (Search for Extraterrestrial Intelligence) focuses on detecting possible signals from other worlds. METI takes the opposite approach: instead of waiting for a message, humans intentionally transmit one. The argument from METI supporters is that a civilisation capable of detecting radio signals may already be looking for signs of technological life, and that communication requires both a listener and a speaker.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://iaaseti.org/en/san-marino-scale/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: iaaseti.org">[IAASeti]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">iaaseti.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">San Marino Scale</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">San Marino Scale...</span></span></span>

Several advocates argue that refusing to transmit creates an uneven relationship with the cosmos. Humanity has already been studying the skies for signs of others, and some scientists argue that an active search naturally raises the question of whether Earth should also make itself known. The astronomer Alexander Zaitsev, a prominent supporter of METI, argued that searching and messaging are closely connected because both activities involve identifying likely locations of technological civilisations and considering how interstellar communication might work.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/0711.2368" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Sending and Searching for Interstellar Messages</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">arXiv Sending and Searching for Interstellar Messages</span></span></span>

For supporters, the potential rewards are significant. A successful exchange could provide knowledge about biology, technology, philosophy or the long-term survival of intelligent life. Some researchers see contact not as a threat scenario but as a possible opportunity for a civilisation with greater experience to share information about avoiding self-destruction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/0711.2368" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Sending and Searching for Interstellar Messages</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">arXiv Sending and Searching for Interstellar Messages</span></span></span>

### The messages already sent

Humanity has already carried out several deliberate transmissions, although none was intended as a realistic two-way conversation in the near future. The best-known example is the 1974 Arecibo Message, sent from the Arecibo Observatory towards the globular cluster Messier 13. The binary message included information about humanity, DNA, the Solar System and the telescope used to send it. Because Messier 13 is about 25,000 light-years away, any reply would take tens of thousands of years even if it were received and answered immediately.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/arecibo-message-alien-extraterrestrial-communication" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalgeographic.com">[National Geographic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalgeographic.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National GeographicWhat you didn&#x27;t know about the first time we tried to contact aliens &#124; National GeographicNovember 14, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 14, 2024</span></span></span>

More recent METI efforts have attempted more targeted communication. In 2017, METI International transmitted a message towards the nearby potentially habitable exoplanet GJ 273b, also known as Luyten b. The project used mathematics and music as possible foundations for communication, reflecting a central METI challenge: how can humans create a message that an entirely different intelligence could interpret?<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/story/metis-first-message-is-a-music-lesson-for-aliens" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">METI&#x27;s First Message Is a Music Lesson for Aliens</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The transmission, originating from a radio telescope in Tromsø, Norway, was designed to teach aliens about music using a binary system. These projects reveal that METI is not simply about saying “hello”. It involves decisions about language, symbolism, cultural representation and whether humans can design a message that is meaningful beyond Earth.

## Concerns about unknown civilisations

### The risk of announcing ourselves

The strongest criticism of METI is based on uncertainty. Humans have no evidence about the intentions, behaviour or capabilities of extraterrestrial civilisations. Critics argue that sending a powerful, deliberate signal creates a risk that cannot be evaluated because humanity has no examples of other technological species.

This concern became widely known through warnings from figures such as Stephen Hawking, who argued that contact with a much more advanced civilisation might resemble historical encounters between technologically unequal human societies. Hawking suggested that humanity should be cautious about making itself visible because a more advanced civilisation’s motivations could be impossible to predict.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/stephen-hawking-controversial-physics-black-holes-bets-science" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalgeographic.com">[National Geographic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalgeographic.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National GeographicControversial Stephen Hawking Predictions, From Evil Aliens to Black Hole Bets &#124; National GeographicMay 2, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 2, 2018</span></span></span>

The criticism does not require assuming aliens would be hostile. Even a neutral or indifferent civilisation could create consequences that humanity cannot control. A message revealing Earth’s existence, technology level or biological information might have effects that cannot be reversed once transmitted.

### Who gets to speak for Earth?

Another major dispute concerns authority. A message from Earth is not merely a scientific experiment; it represents an entire planet. Critics argue that a small group of researchers, institutions or private organisations should not make a decision with potentially global consequences without wider public involvement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0094576520307293" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect SETI and democracy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SETI and democracy - ScienceDirectMarch 1, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 1, 2021</span></span></span>

This issue has become a central part of space-policy discussions. The International Academy of Astronautics has developed frameworks for considering communication with extraterrestrial intelligence, while researchers have proposed methods for evaluating the possible impact of transmissions. The San Marino Scale, developed within the SETI community, attempts to assess the potential significance of messages sent from Earth by considering factors such as signal strength and information content.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://iaaseti.org/en/san-marino-scale/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: iaaseti.org">[IAASeti]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">iaaseti.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">San Marino Scale</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">San Marino Scale...</span></span></span>

The disagreement is therefore not only scientific but political: if humanity ever chooses to speak to another civilisation, the question is who has the legitimacy to make that decision.

## The science fiction debate

Science fiction has shaped the METI argument because it provides imagined examples of both possibilities: contact as a breakthrough and contact as a catastrophe. Stories about alien invasion often begin with the assumption that being discovered is dangerous, while peaceful-contact narratives imagine communication as a way to overcome fear and isolation.

A modern example of this tension is the popularity of “dark forest” ideas, especially through Liu Cixin’s *The [Three-Body]({{ 'three-body/' | relative_url }}) Problem* series, which portrays the universe as a place where revealing one’s location could be dangerous. Although fictional, such stories have influenced public discussion by giving a dramatic form to the real scientific uncertainty behind METI debates.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wsj.com/science/humanity-shouldnt-be-afraid-to-say-hello-to-aliens-702c8fd8" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wsj.com">[The Wall Street Journal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wsj.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Wall Street Journal Humanity Shouldn&#x27;t Be Afraid to Say Hello to Aliens</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The notion gained attention due to Netflix&#x27;s series &quot;3 Body Problem,&quot; based on Liu Cixin&#x27;s novels, which depict catastrophic consequences...</span></span></span>

Other science-fiction traditions take the opposite view. Stories influenced by works such as *Contact* imagine communication with extraterrestrial intelligence as a scientific and cultural achievement. In this view, refusing to communicate could mean missing one of the greatest discoveries possible for a technological civilisation.

The lasting value of science fiction in this debate is not that it predicts what aliens would do. Instead, it helps explore human choices: whether curiosity should overcome fear, whether caution should limit exploration, and whether humanity sees itself as a participant in a wider cosmic community or as a civilisation that should remain hidden.

## A cautious path between silence and contact

The METI debate has not produced a universal agreement. Supporters argue that a silent humanity may miss opportunities for discovery, cooperation and knowledge. Critics argue that deliberate transmissions create risks without any guaranteed benefit. Both sides face the same fundamental problem: humanity is making decisions without knowing what exists beyond Earth.

A practical compromise discussed within the SETI community is greater international discussion before powerful transmissions are sent. Rather than treating METI as either reckless exposure or an unavoidable next step, many researchers argue that communication decisions should involve scientific, ethical and public debate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://iaaseti.org/en/protocols/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: iaaseti.org">[IAASeti]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">iaaseti.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Protocols...</span></span></span>

The question of sending messages to aliens therefore reflects a broader question about humanity itself. [First contact]({{ 'first-contact/' | relative_url }}), if it ever occurs, will not only reveal something about another civilisation. It will reveal how humans respond when curiosity, ambition and uncertainty collide.
