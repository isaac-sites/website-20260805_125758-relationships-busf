## How television changed the UFO narrative

Early UFO-themed television often reflected the anxieties of the Cold War. Programmes used alien visitors to explore fears about invasion, nuclear weapons, [military technology]({{ 'military-technology/' | relative_url }}) and humanity’s vulnerability. Rather than simply presenting aliens as monsters, television frequently turned UFOs into symbols for wider social concerns: mistrust of authority, uncertainty about technological progress and questions about humanity’s place in the universe.

A significant example was UFO, created by Gerry and Sylvia Anderson. The series imagined a secret international defence organisation protecting Earth from alien attacks, combining futuristic technology with a military response to extraterrestrial threats. Its approach reflected a recurring television theme: UFOs were not merely strange objects but challenges requiring investigation, surveillance and institutional action.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UFO_%28British_TV_series%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UFO (British TV series</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO (British TV series</span></span></span>

Television also benefited from the episodic format because UFO stories naturally depend on mystery. Each episode could introduce a new sighting, witness or unexplained event while allowing the wider series to develop questions about alien motives and human responses. This structure encouraged viewers to consider competing explanations rather than presenting a single interpretation.

The most influential television treatment of UFOs arrived with The X-Files, which ran from 1993 to 2002. The series changed UFO storytelling by combining investigative drama, science fiction and conspiracy narratives. ## When real UFO investigations became television stories

Television frequently borrowed authority from real UFO investigations, especially government programmes such as the United States Air Force’s Project Blue Book. These investigations provided writers with official documents, reported [sightings]({{ 'sightings/' | relative_url }}) and historical controversies that could be adapted into fictional narratives.

The 1978 series Project U.F.O. drew directly from Project Blue Book, using fictionalised Air Force investigators who examined reported UFO incidents. The programme reflected a period when public interest in UFOs was high and when television increasingly blurred the boundary between documentary-style investigation and fictional drama.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_U.F.O" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project U.F.O</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project U.F.O</span></span></span>

## Television’s unique power: making uncertainty personal

The strongest UFO television stories rarely depend only on the existence of aliens. Their lasting appeal comes from showing how individuals respond when faced with something they cannot explain. Television places viewers alongside investigators, witnesses and families, making the emotional experience of uncertainty part of the story.

This differs from many UFO films, where the focus is often on spectacle or direct confrontation. Television can explore slower questions: What happens when a witness is ignored? How should scientists investigate unusual claims? Can governments be trusted with unknown information? These questions allow UFO stories to connect with broader science-fiction themes about knowledge, technology and society.

Series such as *The X-Files* also showed that UFO storytelling could move beyond simple invasion narratives. Alien encounters became a framework for discussing ethics, identity, political power and the relationship between evidence and belief. This helped shift science fiction television from stories about external threats towards stories about human interpretation of the unknown.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.si.edu/newsdesk/releases/smithsonian-wants-believe-national-museum-american-history-acquires-x-files-collection" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: si.edu">[Smithsonian Institution]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Smithsonian InstitutionSmithsonian Wants to Believe! ## From flying saucers to modern UAP storytelling

Contemporary television has continued to adapt UFO themes as public language around the subject has changed. Older programmes often used “flying saucers” and alien visitation as their central ideas, while newer productions frequently use the term UAP (unidentified anomalous phenomena), reflecting more recent official discussions of unexplained aerial observations.

The continuing popularity of UFO-related television shows that the subject remains useful for science fiction because it sits between certainty and speculation. A UFO story can examine advanced technology, possible [extraterrestrial life]({{ 'extraterrestrial-life/' | relative_url }}), government secrecy or human psychology without requiring a final answer. Television’s long-form structure makes that ambiguity especially effective, allowing audiences to revisit the same fundamental question across generations: how should humanity respond when it encounters something it does not yet understand?
