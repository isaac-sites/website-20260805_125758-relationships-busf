## Introduction

Long before the modern flying saucer became a [science-fiction]({{ 'science-fiction/' | relative_url }}) icon, artists were already using unusual things in the sky to represent mystery, danger, divine power and events beyond ordinary understanding. Medieval and Renaissance paintings, illustrated chronicles and early printed broadsheets did not depict “UFOs” in the modern sense, but they created a visual vocabulary that later influenced how unfamiliar objects above the Earth would be imagined. Glowing discs, celestial lights, hovering forms and dramatic sky events became recurring ways to show contact with forces outside everyday experience.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metabunk.org/ufoarthistory/about.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metabunk.org">[Metabunk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metabunk.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">About — UFO Art History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">About — UFO Art History...</span></span></span>

When 20th-century science fiction began creating its own UFO designs, it drew on centuries of visual traditions in which the sky was a place where the unknown could appear.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metabunk.org/ufoarthistory/about.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metabunk.org">[Metabunk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metabunk.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">About — UFO Art History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">About — UFO Art History...</span></span></span>

## Earlier mysterious sky scenes

### Strange objects were usually signs, not machines

Before the modern concept of extraterrestrial vehicles existed, European artists commonly portrayed unusual sky events as religious signs, warnings or wonders. The same visual features later associated with [UFO imagery]({{ 'ufo-imagery/' | relative_url }}) — circles, spheres, beams of light and objects floating above landscapes — had different meanings in their original settings. Art historians describe medieval and Renaissance imagery as a symbolic language in which viewers were expected to recognise theological meanings rather than interpret every unusual shape literally.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metabunk.org/ufoarthistory/about.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metabunk.org">[Metabunk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metabunk.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">About — UFO Art History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">About — UFO Art History...</span></span></span>

A major example is the tradition of depicting heavenly appearances in Christian art. Scenes of the Annunciation often show light descending from heaven towards the Virgin Mary, representing divine intervention rather than a physical object. In Carlo Crivelli’s 1486 painting *The Annunciation, with Saint Emidius*, a beam of heavenly light enters the scene through an opening in the sky as part of the religious narrative. Modern viewers sometimes compare the shape to a flying saucer, but within Renaissance visual culture it represented the Holy Spirit and the sacred event being depicted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalgallery.org.uk/paintings/carlo-crivelli-the-annunciation-with-saint-emidius?style=accessible" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalgallery.org.uk">[National Gallery]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalgallery.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationalgallery.org.uk.</span></span></span>

This difference in interpretation explains why some historical paintings are repeatedly reintroduced into UFO discussions. The images contain forms that resemble modern expectations — a bright object overhead, a directed beam, a dramatic arrival — but their original audiences understood them through religious symbolism rather than ideas of space travel.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metabunk.org/ufoarthistory/about.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metabunk.org">[Metabunk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metabunk.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">About — UFO Art History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">About — UFO Art History...</span></span></span>

### The 1561 Nuremberg sky event became a bridge to UFO imagery

The most influential historical image in modern UFO culture is Hans Glaser’s 1561 woodcut of the Nuremberg celestial phenomenon. The image shows numerous geometric forms above the city, including spheres, cylindrical shapes and cross-like objects. The accompanying account described a terrifying spectacle in the sky and presented it as a warning from God, fitting the early modern tradition of illustrated “wonders” that recorded unusual events as moral or spiritual messages.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metabunk.org/ufoarthistory/painting.html?id=nuremberg-1561" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metabunk.org">[Metabunk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metabunk.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Celestial Phenomenon over Nuremberg — UFO Art History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Celestial Phenomenon over Nuremberg — UFO Art History...</span></span></span>

The image later became popular among UFO writers because its forms resemble modern descriptions of aerial objects. However, historians and atmospheric researchers have generally interpreted the event differently. The scene is consistent with complex optical phenomena such as halos around the Sun, including parhelia (bright spots created by sunlight interacting with ice crystals in the atmosphere). The artistic representation transformed a natural sky display into a dramatic narrative of conflict and warning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metabunk.org/ufoarthistory/painting.html?id=nuremberg-1561" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metabunk.org">[Metabunk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metabunk.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Celestial Phenomenon over Nuremberg — UFO Art History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Celestial Phenomenon over Nuremberg — UFO Art History...</span></span></span>

The importance of the Nuremberg image for UFO history is therefore cultural rather than evidential. It demonstrates a long-standing human habit: when confronted with an unusual sky, people create images that reflect the fears, beliefs and technologies of their own era. In the 16th century the language was divine signs; in the 20th century it became alien spacecraft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metabunk.org/ufoarthistory/painting.html?id=nuremberg-1561" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metabunk.org">[Metabunk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metabunk.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Celestial Phenomenon over Nuremberg — UFO Art History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Celestial Phenomenon over Nuremberg — UFO Art History...</span></span></span>

## Religious and supernatural meanings

### The sky as a place of revelation

For many centuries, the heavens were understood as a boundary between the human world and higher powers. Artists used unusual sky imagery to communicate encounters with angels, miracles, judgement or cosmic order. This tradition created visual habits that later science fiction adapted: something arrives from above, is difficult to understand and changes humanity’s perspective.

Medieval and Renaissance depictions of celestial events often combined realism with symbolism. A comet, halo, unusual cloud formation or beam of light could be recorded because it was visually striking, but its meaning came from contemporary religious interpretation. Historical reports of atmospheric displays were frequently connected to ideas of divine approval, punishment or prophecy rather than physical explanations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metabunk.org/ufoarthistory/about.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metabunk.org">[Metabunk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metabunk.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">About — UFO Art History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">About — UFO Art History...</span></span></span>

The result was a powerful artistic pattern: the unknown comes from the sky. This pattern survived even after scientific astronomy changed public understanding of the heavens. When science fiction replaced angels and divine signs with visitors from other worlds, it retained the same basic composition — humanity looking upward at something unfamiliar.

### From celestial wonders to imagined visitors

The transition from religious sky imagery to UFO imagery happened gradually. Early modern artists were not imagining alien spacecraft, but their compositions established several features that later became central to UFO art:

* **The descending object:** A heavenly presence entering the human world became a template later reused for spacecraft arriving from space.
* **The beam or glow:** Divine rays and supernatural illumination influenced later depictions of energy beams, landing lights and mysterious emissions.
* **The witness below:** Many historical images show humans reacting to a sky event, a structure later repeated in science fiction scenes of [first contact]({{ 'first-contact/' | relative_url }}).
* **The dramatic sky:** Clouds, darkness and unusual lighting became visual signals that something extraordinary was happening.

These similarities explain why UFO imagery often feels older than the modern UFO itself. [The flying saucer]({{ 'the-flying-saucer/' | relative_url }}) is a recent design, but the idea of an unexplained presence appearing overhead is part of a much older artistic tradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metabunk.org/ufoarthistory/about.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metabunk.org">[Metabunk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metabunk.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">About — UFO Art History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">About — UFO Art History...</span></span></span>

## Links to modern UFO visuals

### Why old sky imagery shaped science fiction

When UFOs entered popular culture in the 20th century, artists and filmmakers inherited centuries of imagery built around mysterious things in the sky. The modern spacecraft did not emerge from a blank canvas; it developed from earlier ways of representing the unknown.

The classic UFO image — a bright disc hovering above a landscape — works because it combines familiar historical ideas with modern technology. The disc resembles earlier celestial symbols, while its metallic surface and mechanical appearance reflect the industrial age. Science fiction transformed spiritual mystery into technological mystery.

This change can be seen in the shift from religious wonder to scientific speculation. Earlier images asked what supernatural force might be acting above humanity. Modern [UFO artwork]({{ 'ufo-artwork/' | relative_url }}) asks what intelligence or technology might exist beyond Earth. The emotional structure remains similar: curiosity, uncertainty and the possibility of contact with something greater than ourselves.

### Historical imagery and the flying saucer tradition

The flying saucer became dominant after the 1940s and 1950s, but its visual impact depended partly on older habits of imagining strange things above the horizon. Artists designing spacecraft for magazines, films and illustrations used smooth shapes, unusual lighting and dramatic scale because these qualities immediately communicated mystery.

The influence works both ways. Modern UFO culture has also changed how people view older artworks. A medieval beam of light or Renaissance heavenly symbol may now be interpreted by some audiences through the lens of space travel, even when the original meaning was religious. This process shows how images gain new meanings as cultural ideas change.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metabunk.org/ufoarthistory/about.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metabunk.org">[Metabunk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metabunk.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">About — UFO Art History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">About — UFO Art History...</span></span></span>

Historical sky images therefore occupy an important place between religion, art and science fiction. They did not predict UFOs, but they helped establish the visual language that made UFOs believable as cultural symbols: something unknown appears in the sky, humans observe it, and the meaning of the event depends on the worldview of the people interpreting it.
