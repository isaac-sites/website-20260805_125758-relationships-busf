## Introduction

Television news has shaped UFO belief formation less by proving or disproving extraterrestrial claims than by deciding which [sightings]({{ 'sightings/' | relative_url }}), witnesses and explanations become publicly important. News programmes transform ambiguous observations into narratives: a report can present a sighting as a possible scientific mystery, a security issue, a mistake requiring explanation, or evidence of official secrecy. Research on television news effects suggests that these framing choices can influence how audiences interpret UFO claims, although exposure alone does not determine belief.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/01463379809370102" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tandfonline.com">[Taylor &amp; Francis Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tandfonline.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Taylor &amp; Francis OnlineDoes television news about UFOs affect viewers’ UFO beliefs?: An experimental investigation: Communication Quarter...</span></span></span>

## News formats and UFO claims

Television news gives UFO stories a distinctive form because it combines the authority of journalism with the uncertainty of unexplained events. A fictional film clearly signals imagination, but a news segment uses familiar markers of factual reporting: interviews, expert commentary, official statements and eyewitness testimony. These elements can make an uncertain claim appear more socially significant even when the evidence remains unresolved.

### From sightings to public controversies

Television coverage often determines which parts of a UFO report become central. A sighting may involve an unusual light, an unclear video or a military observation, but news coverage must select a focus. Reports centred on witnesses can emphasise personal experience and mystery; reports centred on scientists can emphasise evidence and uncertainty; reports centred on governments can shift attention towards secrecy and trust.

This selection process became especially important as UFO discussions moved from older “flying saucer” stories towards modern unidentified aerial phenomena (UAP) reporting. Public interest increased when military encounters and government investigations received mainstream attention, but the existence of official interest did not itself establish an extraterrestrial explanation. Surveys show that many Americans believe officials know more about UFOs than they publicly disclose, while a smaller share conclude that UFOs are alien spacecraft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://news.gallup.com/poll/266441/americans-skeptical-ufos-say-government-knows.aspx" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: news.gallup.com">[Gallup.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">news.gallup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">americans skeptical ufos say government knows.aspx</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Skeptical of UFOs, but Say Government Knows MoreSeptember 6, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 6, 2019</span></span></span>

### The role of experts and official voices

One of the clearest findings from media-effects research is that expert framing can change how viewers evaluate UFO claims. Sparks, Pellechia and Irvine tested television news reports that differed in whether scientific authority was used to challenge UFO interpretations. Their findings indicated that the presence and direction of expert commentary affected subsequent UFO beliefs: reports containing scientific criticism reduced acceptance of UFO claims compared with less critical versions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/01463379809370102" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tandfonline.com">[Taylor &amp; Francis Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tandfonline.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Taylor &amp; Francis OnlineDoes television news about UFOs affect viewers’ UFO beliefs?: An experimental investigation: Communication Quarter...</span></span></span>

Earlier research examining news stories also found that scientific voices acted as important signals for audiences. When a scientist in a news report supported the possibility of UFO reality, participants became more likely to express belief; when scientific authority challenged the claims, the effects were more complicated and did not always produce equal levels of scepticism.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/08934219709367672" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tandfonline.com">[Taylor &amp; Francis Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tandfonline.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Taylor &amp; Francis OnlineThe effect of news stories about UFOs on readers&#x27; UFO beliefs: The role of confirming or disconfirming testimony f...</span></span></span> This suggests that television news does not simply transmit information but also provides audiences with cues about which interpretations appear credible.

## Audience reactions to coverage

Television coverage can influence belief, but it works within existing attitudes, expectations and cultural narratives. Viewers do not approach UFO stories as blank slates. Some already interpret unusual events through ideas about [extraterrestrial life]({{ 'extraterrestrial-life/' | relative_url }}), government secrecy or scientific uncertainty, while others begin from a sceptical position.

### Exposure does not create one shared belief

Public opinion data shows the limits of media influence. A Gallup survey found that in 2019 around one-third of Americans believed some UFO sightings were alien spacecraft, while a larger group thought UFO reports could be explained by human activity or natural phenomena. At the same time, many respondents believed that the government possessed more information about UFOs than it had released publicly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://news.gallup.com/poll/266441/americans-skeptical-ufos-say-government-knows.aspx" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: news.gallup.com">[Gallup.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">news.gallup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">americans skeptical ufos say government knows.aspx</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Skeptical of UFOs, but Say Government Knows MoreSeptember 6, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 6, 2019</span></span></span>

This gap is important because it shows that media coverage may strengthen interest in UFO debates without producing a single conclusion. A viewer may become more interested in a military sighting after seeing television coverage while remaining uncertain about whether the explanation is extraterrestrial, technological or ordinary.

### Mystery, trust and the power of repeated coverage

Repeated television attention can make UFO topics more culturally familiar. When audiences repeatedly encounter reports involving witnesses, officials and unexplained footage, UFOs become established as a legitimate subject of public discussion even when individual cases remain disputed.

However, familiarity is not the same as confirmation. The same media attention that increases awareness can also expose audiences to competing explanations. A programme that presents eyewitness testimony alongside scientific analysis may encourage uncertainty rather than belief. ## Limits of media influence on belief

Television news can shape attention and interpretation, but it is only one factor in UFO belief formation. Personal experiences, cultural expectations, trust in institutions and broader science attitudes also influence how people evaluate UFO claims.

### Media effects are strongest at the level of interpretation

The most defensible conclusion from research is that television news affects how UFO reports are understood rather than directly causing belief. Coverage can make a sighting seem more mysterious, more credible, more doubtful or more connected to government secrecy depending on the voices and evidence presented.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://web.ics.purdue.edu/~sparks/ufonews.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: web.ics.purdue.edu">[Purdue ICS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">web.ics.purdue.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Purdue ICSUntitled...</span></span></span>

This explains why the same UFO report can produce different audience reactions. One viewer may focus on the existence of an unexplained observation, while another focuses on missing evidence or conventional explanations. News framing provides a pathway for interpretation, but audiences still filter reports through their own assumptions.

### Why UFO coverage remains contested

The challenge for television journalism is balancing public interest with evidential standards. UFO reports often involve incomplete information, unusual eyewitness accounts and rapidly changing explanations. Dramatic presentation may increase attention, but it can also blur the distinction between an unidentified event and evidence for extraterrestrial visitation.

Modern UFO coverage continues to reflect this tension. Government agencies and scientific organisations have increased efforts to collect and analyse reports, while public debate continues over transparency and explanation. The result is a media environment where television remains influential in setting the agenda of UFO discussions, but belief depends on a wider relationship between evidence, trust and interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://news.gallup.com/poll/350096/americans-believe-ufos.aspx" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: news.gallup.com">[Gallup.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">news.gallup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">americans believe ufos.aspx</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Americans Believe in UFOs?...</span></span></span>
