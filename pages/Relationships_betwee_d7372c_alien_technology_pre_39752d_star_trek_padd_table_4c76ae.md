## Introduction

The Star Trek Personal Access Display Device (PADD) is one of the clearest examples of [science fiction]({{ 'science-fiction/' | relative_url }}) anticipating a future style of computing rather than predicting a specific commercial product. First seen in the Star Trek universe as a portable information terminal, the PADD presented a vision of a thin, handheld device for reading documents, reviewing data and interacting with computer systems. Decades later, tablet computers such as the iPad made many of those everyday interactions familiar. The connection is not that Star Trek directly invented the tablet, but that it helped popularise the idea of computing as something personal, portable and centred around information access.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arstechnica.com/gadgets/2016/09/how-star-trek-artists-imagined-the-ipad-23-years-ago/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arstechnica.com">[Ars Technica]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arstechnica.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ars TechnicaHow Star Trek artists imagined the iPad... nearly 30 years ago - Ars TechnicaSeptember 10, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 10, 2016</span></span></span>

## The Star Trek Tablet Before Tablets

### The PADD as portable knowledge device

The PADD, especially in Star Trek: The Next Generation and later series, was designed as a compact computer interface used throughout everyday life aboard starships and Federation facilities. Characters used PADDs to read reports, review technical information, complete administrative tasks and access stored knowledge. Rather than presenting computers as large machines located in dedicated rooms, Star Trek showed computing becoming a normal personal object carried by individuals.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arstechnica.com/gadgets/2016/09/how-star-trek-artists-imagined-the-ipad-23-years-ago/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arstechnica.com">[Ars Technica]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arstechnica.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ars TechnicaHow Star Trek artists imagined the iPad... nearly 30 years ago - Ars TechnicaSeptember 10, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 10, 2016</span></span></span>

This design choice reflected a broader science-fiction idea: advanced societies would not necessarily interact with computers through increasingly complex machinery, but through simpler interfaces that disappear into ordinary activities. A Starfleet officer could pick up a PADD in the same way a person in the present day picks up a tablet to read an article, check a document or view information. The important prediction was the relationship between user and device.

The production designers behind the PADD also approached it as an evolution of earlier fictional and real-world ideas about portable information tools. Rick Sternbach, one of the designers associated with Star Trek technology, described the PADD as part of a longer tradition of science-fiction data displays rather than a single invention appearing from nowhere. He noted that the device was imagined as a portable, book-sized information system capable of communicating with other technology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.startrek.com/news/illustrator-designer-rick-sternbach-recalls-his-trek-days-part-1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: startrek.com">[Star Trek]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">startrek.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">illustrator designer rick sternbach recalls his trek days part 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Star TrekIllustrator/Designer Rick Sternbach Recalls His Trek Days, Part 1October 27, 2011...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 27, 2011</span></span></span>

The PADD therefore fits into a long history of attempts to imagine a “book of the future”. Earlier concepts such as Alan Kay’s Dynabook proposed a portable personal computer designed around learning and information access, showing that the idea of mobile digital knowledge existed before the modern tablet market.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dynabook.com/global/en/story/ep1/index.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dynabook.com">[us.dynabook.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dynabook.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on dynabook.com.</span></span></span> Star Trek’s contribution was making that concept visible to a large audience through a familiar narrative setting.

## Modern tablet comparisons

### Where PADDs resemble today’s tablets

The similarities between the PADD and modern tablets are strongest at the level of interaction. Both are:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* thin, portable screens rather than traditional desktop computers;
* designed for reading and displaying information;
* used while moving through everyday environments;
* controlled through direct interaction with the display;
* personal devices carried by individuals rather than shared machines.

</div>

When Apple introduced the iPad in 2010, it emphasised many of these same qualities: a thin mobile device with a Multi-Touch display intended for browsing, reading, media consumption and productivity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2010/01/27Apple-Launches-iPad/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apple.com">[Apple]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apple.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Launches i Pad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Apple Launches iPad - AppleJanuary 27, 2010...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 27, 2010</span></span></span> The resemblance between the iPad and Star Trek PADDs became widely discussed because both represented a shift away from keyboard-centred computing towards direct manipulation of digital content.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arstechnica.com/gadgets/2016/09/how-star-trek-artists-imagined-the-ipad-23-years-ago/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arstechnica.com">[Ars Technica]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arstechnica.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ars TechnicaHow Star Trek artists imagined the iPad... nearly 30 years ago - Ars TechnicaSeptember 10, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 10, 2016</span></span></span>

However, the comparison should not be treated as a claim that Apple simply copied Star Trek. Tablet computing had several independent historical influences, including portable computer research, touchscreen development and concepts such as the Dynabook. The PADD was one cultural influence among many because it helped make the idea of portable screen-based computing feel natural and desirable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dynabook.com/global/en/story/ep1/index.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dynabook.com">[us.dynabook.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dynabook.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on dynabook.com.</span></span></span>

### The differences reveal the real prediction

The PADD also differs from modern tablets in important ways. In Star Trek, characters often appeared to use multiple PADDs, each containing specific information. Modern tablets instead combine many functions into one device through software applications, cloud services and internet connectivity. The fictional device was closer to a universal digital clipboard, while modern tablets became general-purpose computing platforms.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.startrek.com/news/illustrator-designer-rick-sternbach-recalls-his-trek-days-part-1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: startrek.com">[Star Trek]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">startrek.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">illustrator designer rick sternbach recalls his trek days part 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Star TrekIllustrator/Designer Rick Sternbach Recalls His Trek Days, Part 1October 27, 2011...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 27, 2011</span></span></span>

This difference highlights why science-fiction predictions should be judged by the behaviour they anticipate rather than the hardware they depict. Star Trek did not predict the exact operating systems, processors or app ecosystems that would define tablets. It predicted that people would want immediate access to information through lightweight personal screens.

## Predicting behaviour over hardware

The lasting influence of the PADD comes from its understanding of how people might live with technology. Before tablets became mainstream, computers were often imagined as devices requiring desks, keyboards and specialised environments. The PADD suggested another possibility: computing could become as casual and mobile as reading a book.

This idea became central to later consumer technology. The modern tablet is not simply a smaller computer; it represents a different relationship with information. People use tablets while travelling, relaxing, studying, presenting work or consuming media. The device fits into existing human activities rather than requiring users to adapt their behaviour around the machine.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2010/01/27Apple-Launches-iPad/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apple.com">[Apple]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apple.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Launches i Pad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Apple Launches iPad - AppleJanuary 27, 2010...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 27, 2010</span></span></span>

The PADD’s influence is therefore best understood as design inspiration and cultural preparation. Science fiction helped audiences imagine that a future computer might be something held in the hand rather than something operated from behind a desk. Designers and engineers later developed the technical foundations that made such devices practical.

Within the broader connection between UFO ideas, imagined [alien technology]({{ 'alien-technology/' | relative_url }}) and science fiction, the PADD represents a grounded form of technological speculation. It does not claim that advanced civilisations would literally use the same devices humans later build. Instead, it shows how imagining sophisticated societies can encourage people to rethink what technology should do: provide knowledge anywhere, reduce barriers between humans and information, and become a natural extension of everyday life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/communication/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2026.1797425/full" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: frontiersin.org">[Frontiers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">frontiersin.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FrontiersFrontiers &#124; Designing the future: Star Trek as visual culture, speculative design, and imagination activator in human–technology...</span></span></span>
