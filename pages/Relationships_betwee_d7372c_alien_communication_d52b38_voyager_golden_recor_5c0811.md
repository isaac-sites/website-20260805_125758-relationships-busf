## Introduction

The Voyager Golden Record was humanity’s attempt to answer a difficult question: how could one civilisation introduce itself to another without sharing a language, history or biology? Attached to NASA’s Voyager 1 and Voyager 2 spacecraft in 1977, the record was designed as a message for a possible extraterrestrial audience, combining scientific diagrams, natural sounds, music, spoken greetings and images of Earth. Its importance in the relationship between UFO ideas and [science fiction]({{ 'science-fiction/' | relative_url }}) lies in the same problem that many fictional [first-contact]({{ 'first-contact/' | relative_url }}) stories explore: the challenge is not simply sending a message, but creating something another intelligence could recognise as meaningful.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/mission/voyager/voyager-golden-record-overview/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science Golden Record Overview</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceGolden Record Overview - NASA ScienceApril 7, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 7, 2025</span></span></span>

The Golden Record was therefore both a communication experiment and a cultural self-portrait. Its designers tried to begin with ideas that might be shared across worlds, such as mathematics, physics and observation of nature, while also including distinctly human experiences such as music and emotion. Yet the project also revealed the limits of any attempt at “universal” communication: every symbol, image and sound still depends on assumptions about how an unknown listener thinks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/mission/voyager/golden-record-contents/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science Golden Record Contents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceGolden Record Contents - NASA ScienceApril 7, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 7, 2025</span></span></span>

## Building a message for another civilisation

The design challenge facing the Golden Record team was unlike ordinary translation. A human message between different nations can rely on some shared foundations: similar senses, a shared planet, knowledge of human biology and familiarity with the idea of language. An extraterrestrial recipient would offer none of those guarantees. The record’s creators therefore attempted to construct a message that could begin from the most basic possible references.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/mission/voyager/voyager-golden-record-overview/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science Golden Record Overview</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceGolden Record Overview - NASA ScienceApril 7, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 7, 2025</span></span></span>

The physical object itself was part of the communication strategy. Each Voyager spacecraft carried a 12-inch gold-plated copper phonograph record, protected by a cover containing instructions intended to explain how the record could be played and interpreted. Rather than assuming an alien civilisation would immediately understand human technology, the cover attempted to provide a decoding pathway through scientific information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/resource/voyager-golden-record/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science Voyager Golden Record</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceVoyager Golden Record - NASA ScienceDecember 4, 2017...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 4, 2017</span></span></span>

### Starting with physics rather than language

One of the central design choices was to avoid beginning with human speech. Spoken languages are highly dependent on cultural learning, so the record began instead with scientific references. The cover includes a diagram based on the transition of the hydrogen atom, used as a reference for time measurement, alongside instructions for interpreting the recording format. The designers hoped that a technologically advanced civilisation would understand fundamental physical processes even if it knew nothing about Earth.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nasa.gov/image-article/voyagers-special-cargo-golden-record/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nasa.gov">[NASA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Voyager&#x27;s Special Cargo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Voyager&#x27;s Special Cargo - The Golden Record - NASA...</span></span></span>

The cover also includes a pulsar map intended to show the location of the Solar System. Pulsars are rapidly rotating neutron stars with distinctive signals, and their patterns can act as a kind of cosmic reference system. The idea was that a civilisation capable of studying the record might compare the pulsar information with astronomical observations and identify where the spacecraft originated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://airandspace.si.edu/multimedia-gallery/web12083-2011640jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airandspace.si.edu">[Smithsonian Air and Space Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airandspace.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Smithsonian Air and Space Museum Voyager Record &#124; National Air and Space Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Smithsonian Air and Space Museum Voyager Record &#124; National Air and Space Museum</span></span></span>

This reflects a major assumption behind many attempts at interstellar communication: mathematics and physics may provide a common foundation because the laws of nature should be observable anywhere in the universe. However, researchers have also pointed out that even scientific communication involves choices about representation, interpretation and the cognitive abilities of the receiver. What humans consider an obvious pattern may not be obvious to a very different intelligence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/0807.4518" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>

## Symbols, sounds and scientific clues

The contents of the Golden Record were chosen to present Earth as both a physical world and a cultural one. NASA describes the record as containing natural sounds, music, greetings in 55 languages and images intended to portray the diversity of life and civilisation on Earth.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/mission/voyager/voyager-golden-record-overview/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science Golden Record Overview</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceGolden Record Overview - NASA ScienceApril 7, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 7, 2025</span></span></span>

The choice to include human culture created a tension at the heart of the project. Scientific information could potentially be decoded through shared observation of the universe, but music, language and images depend much more heavily on experience. A mathematical diagram might communicate a measurement, while a piece of music may communicate emotion only to a listener with comparable ways of hearing and interpreting patterns.

The spoken greetings illustrate this difficulty. The record includes short greetings from many human languages, ranging from ancient languages to modern ones, not because the designers expected an alien listener to understand the words directly, but because the collection demonstrated that Earth contains many forms of communication.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/mission/voyager/golden-record-contents/images/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science Golden Record Images</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceGolden Record Images - NASA Science...</span></span></span> The greetings function less like a dictionary and more like evidence that humanity is a species capable of symbolic exchange.

The music selection created a similar challenge. The record includes works from different cultures and historical periods, attempting to represent humanity through artistic expression rather than through a single national or cultural identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/mission/voyager/golden-record-contents/images/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science Golden Record Images</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceGolden Record Images - NASA Science...</span></span></span> However, the selection process also raised a fundamental question: who gets to decide what represents an entire planet? The record presents a carefully chosen image of humanity, but it cannot contain the full complexity, conflicts and diversity of human societies.

## The limits of universal communication

The greatest limitation of the Golden Record is that it was built around a hypothetical receiver. The designers could prepare a message, but they could not know whether an alien civilisation would perceive the same categories that humans use. A species with different senses, different concepts of time or different forms of intelligence might not interpret images, sounds or symbols in the intended way.

Images provide a clear example of this problem. The record contains diagrams and photographs, but photographs depend on assumptions about vision and representation. Humans understand that a two-dimensional image can represent a three-dimensional object because of experience with pictures, perspective and visual technology. An alien intelligence might need additional explanation before recognising that a pattern of marks represents a physical scene.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/mission/voyager/golden-record-contents/images/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science Golden Record Images</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceGolden Record Images - NASA Science...</span></span></span>

The same issue applies to the idea of a “universal” language. Science fiction often imagines translation systems that can overcome these barriers, but the Golden Record shows why real communication may be much harder. A message does not only require symbols; it requires a shared framework for assigning meaning to those symbols. The record could indicate that humans exist and that humans communicate, but it could not guarantee understanding.

There is also a practical limitation: discovery itself is extremely unlikely. Voyager is travelling through interstellar space, and NASA notes that it would take around 40,000 years before the spacecraft makes a close approach to another planetary system. The Golden Record was therefore never a conventional broadcast intended for immediate contact. It was closer to a time capsule launched into a vast environment, depending on an unlikely future encounter.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/mission/voyager/golden-record-contents/images/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science Golden Record Images</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceGolden Record Images - NASA Science...</span></span></span>

## Why the Golden Record remains important for alien communication


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

The Golden Record’s value is not that it solved the problem of communicating with extraterrestrials. Instead, it made the problem visible. It showed that first contact would involve more than detecting another civilisation; it would require negotiating meaning between minds shaped by completely different environments.

</div>

This is why the project remains closely connected to science fiction’s exploration of alien communication. Stories about extraterrestrial encounters often ask whether humans can recognise intelligence that does not think like us. The Golden Record approached the same question in a practical form: how can we introduce ourselves when we cannot know what another civilisation understands?

Its final message is therefore both ambitious and limited. Humanity attempted to create a bridge across an unimaginable distance, but the bridge could only be built from human materials: human science, human art, human symbols and human assumptions. The record is a message to possible aliens, but it is also a reflection of how humans imagine communication itself.
