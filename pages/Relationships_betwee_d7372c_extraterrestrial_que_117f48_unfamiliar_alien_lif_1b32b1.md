## Introduction

Fictional aliens are most interesting when they stop looking like humans with different skin colours and start challenging the idea of what life, intelligence and communication can be. [Science fiction]({{ 'science-fiction/' | relative_url }}) has used [extraterrestrial life]({{ 'extraterrestrial-life/' | relative_url }}) forms as experiments in imagination: a planet-sized organism, a distributed consciousness, a species that experiences time differently, or an intelligence that exists through ecosystems rather than individual bodies. These creations matter because they test a central question behind UFO stories and science fiction alike: if humanity encountered another form of life, would we even recognise it as alive or intelligent?

Modern science fiction increasingly moves beyond the familiar humanoid alien. Works such as Stanisław Lem’s *[Solaris]({{ 'solaris/' | relative_url }})*, Ted Chiang’s *Story of Your Life* and its film adaptation *Arrival*, and Jeff VanderMeer’s *Annihilation* imagine beings whose biology and thought processes do not fit human expectations. Their importance is not that they predict real aliens, but that they explore the limits of human understanding. This connects closely with astrobiology, where researchers also recognise that life elsewhere may not resemble Earth organisms in obvious ways and that scientists must avoid assuming humanity represents the universal model for life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/astrobiology/program-overview/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science Astrobiology Program Overview</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceAstrobiology Program Overview - NASA ScienceJune 3, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 3, 2026</span></span></span>

## Beyond humanoid aliens

For much of science fiction history, alien creatures were designed around human bodies. They walked upright, communicated through speech, formed societies similar to human ones and often represented familiar ideas such as invasion, friendship or rivalry. H. G. Wells’s *[The War]({{ 'the-war/' | relative_url }}) of the Worlds* already moved beyond a simple human-like visitor by depicting Martians as radically different organisms with unfamiliar anatomy and technology, helping establish the idea that extraterrestrial life could challenge human assumptions rather than simply mirror them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hachette.co.uk/titles/h-g-wells-6/the-war-of-the-worlds/9780751574760/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hachette.co.uk">[Hachette UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hachette.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the war of the worlds</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hachette UKThe War of the Worlds by H. G. Wells &#124; Hachette UKNovember 14, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 14, 2019</span></span></span>

Later fiction pushed this further by asking whether intelligence requires a recognisable body at all.

### The living world of *Solaris*

Stanisław Lem’s 1961 novel *Solaris* remains one of the strongest examples of a truly unfamiliar alien intelligence. Instead of presenting an alien creature that walks, speaks or builds machines, the novel focuses on a mysterious ocean covering an entire planet. Scientists debate whether this ocean is a single enormous living intelligence, but their attempts to communicate fail because they cannot determine whether its actions are messages, automatic processes or something beyond human interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.newyorker.com/culture/culture-desk/the-beautiful-mind-bending-of-stanislaw-lem" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: newyorker.com">[The New Yorker]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">newyorker.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The New Yorker The Beautiful Mind-Bending of Stanislaw Lem</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">This blending of fiction with reality is a recurring theme in Lem&#x27;s works, notably in his novel &quot;Solaris,&quot; which explores a sentient ocea...</span></span></span>

The importance of *Solaris* is that it changes the meaning of “[first contact]({{ 'first-contact/' | relative_url }})”. A meeting with alien life does not necessarily involve two individuals exchanging words. Contact might involve trying to understand an entity with a different scale of existence, different senses and different concepts of purpose. The story suggests that humanity’s greatest obstacle may not be distance between worlds, but the assumption that another intelligence must think in human categories.

### Life without familiar bodies

Other fictional aliens challenge the idea that an organism must have a single physical form. Science fiction has imagined:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Collective minds**, where intelligence emerges from many connected organisms rather than one individual.
* **Planetary intelligences**, where an entire world or ecosystem functions as a living entity.
* **Shape-changing life forms**, where identity is based on information, adaptation or transformation rather than a fixed body.
* **Non-biological intelligences**, where consciousness develops through artificial or exotic forms of existence.

</div>

These concepts are fictional, but they connect with real scientific questions about how broad the definition of life might become. Astrobiology examines life’s origins, evolution and possible distribution beyond Earth, including environments very different from modern Earth. Researchers study extreme environments on our own planet because they reveal how adaptable life can be.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/biological-physical/stories/space-biology-and-astrobiology-whats-the-difference/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science Space Biology and Astrobiology: What’s the difference?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceSpace Biology and Astrobiology: What’s the difference? - NASA ScienceAugust 3, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 3, 2023</span></span></span>

## Alternative forms of intelligence

The most challenging fictional aliens are often not the strangest-looking ones, but the ones whose minds work differently from ours. Science fiction uses these beings to explore whether intelligence must involve language, technology, emotion or individual identity.

### The communication problem in *Arrival*

Denis Villeneuve’s *Arrival*, adapted from Ted Chiang’s short story *Story of Your Life*, focuses on aliens known as heptapods whose communication system is unlike human language. The story follows linguist Louise Banks as she attempts to understand their written symbols and avoid dangerous misunderstandings during first contact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/story/arrival-science-fact-fiction/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arrival science fact fiction</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The science of Arrival: what the film got right (and wrong) &#124; WIREDNovember 12, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 12, 2016</span></span></span>

The heptapods are significant because they are not presented mainly as invaders or allies. Their unfamiliarity creates a scientific problem: humans must first understand how the aliens organise information before they can understand their intentions. The story treats language as a window into a different way of thinking rather than merely a tool for exchanging words.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/story/arrival-science-fact-fiction/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arrival science fact fiction</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The science of Arrival: what the film got right (and wrong) &#124; WIREDNovember 12, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 12, 2016</span></span></span>

This approach reflects a wider theme in alien fiction: intelligence may not share human priorities. A species might value cooperation, survival, creativity or knowledge in ways that humans cannot immediately recognise. The challenge of first contact would therefore involve interpretation, not just translation.

### Intelligence beyond individuals

Many fictional life forms question the assumption that intelligence belongs to separate individuals. A human perspective usually begins with the idea of a person: one body, one mind and one identity. Alien fiction often removes those boundaries.

Examples include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--checklist" markdown="1">

* A civilisation where individuals are temporary parts of a larger consciousness.
* Organisms that communicate through chemical signals, environmental changes or shared memory.
* Species that operate over centuries because their bodies or societies change extremely slowly.
* Minds that perceive time differently from humans.

</div>

These ideas are valuable because they expose a limitation in human thinking. On Earth, intelligence already appears in many forms, from social insect colonies to complex animal behaviour, even though none perfectly matches human consciousness. Fiction extends that uncertainty into imagined worlds and asks what other patterns intelligence could take.

## Searching for unfamiliar life

Science fiction often influences how people imagine the search for extraterrestrial life, but the relationship also works in the other direction: discoveries about Earth and other worlds expand the range of alien possibilities writers explore.

### From Earth-like planets to stranger possibilities

The scientific search for life beyond Earth does not assume that another planet must contain creatures resembling humans. NASA’s astrobiology programmes study how life emerged and evolved on Earth while investigating where life might exist elsewhere, including environments such as Mars and icy moons with possible subsurface oceans.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nasa.gov/missions/nasas-search-for-life-astrobiology-in-the-solar-system-and-beyond/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nasa.gov">[NASA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">’s Search for Life: Astrobiology in the Solar System and Beyond</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA’s Search for Life: Astrobiology in the Solar System and Beyond - NASAJune 25, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2021</span></span></span>

This uncertainty creates space for fiction to explore unfamiliar possibilities. A planet with different chemistry, gravity, atmosphere or history could produce organisms unlike anything humans have encountered. Even Earth itself demonstrates that life can occupy extreme environments and follow many evolutionary paths.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/biological-physical/stories/space-biology-and-astrobiology-whats-the-difference/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science Space Biology and Astrobiology: What’s the difference?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceSpace Biology and Astrobiology: What’s the difference? - NASA ScienceAugust 3, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 3, 2023</span></span></span>

### The alien as a test of human assumptions

A major theme across fictional alien life forms is that humans may be limited by their own biology and culture when imagining the universe. A recent discussion in astrobiology and related fields has considered whether intelligence might leave signs that are not obvious technological signals, expanding ideas about what evidence of another intelligence could look like.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.space.com/space-exploration/search-for-life/are-we-overlooking-signs-of-alien-life-that-arent-technological-or-biological-bread-would-be-a-signature-of-intelligence" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: space.com">[Space]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">space.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Are we overlooking signs of alien life that aren&#x27;t technological or biological?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bread would be a signature of intelligence&#x27;July 27, 2026 — This article explores expanding the search for extraterrestrial intelligence...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 27, 2026</span></span></span>

Science fiction uses this uncertainty creatively. A fictional alien does not need to be a green humanoid, a monster or a superior civilisation. It can be something that forces humans to rethink the boundaries between life, intelligence and communication.

The most memorable alien species are therefore not necessarily the most powerful or frightening. They are the ones that make familiar concepts unstable: a mind without a body, a society without individuals, a language without human assumptions, or a living system so different that understanding it becomes the central adventure. Fiction’s greatest contribution to extraterrestrial questions is not imagining what aliens might look like, but asking what humanity might fail to recognise when it finally encounters something truly unfamiliar.
