## Introduction

Mathematics is one of [science fiction]({{ 'science-fiction/' | relative_url }})’s most enduring answers to a difficult question: if humans encountered an alien civilisation, what could both sides understand? The idea behind mathematical communication is that numbers, patterns and physical relationships might not depend on human culture or spoken language. A civilisation orbiting another star might not share our biology, history or vocabulary, but it may still recognise repeated structures, quantities and logical relationships. This has made mathematics a recurring bridge between humans and imagined extraterrestrials, from coded radio messages to fictional [first-contact]({{ 'first-contact/' | relative_url }}) stories.

The concept has also influenced real attempts to design messages for possible extraterrestrial intelligence. Projects such as the 1974 Arecibo Message and theoretical systems such as Hans Freudenthal’s Lincos language attempted to begin communication from the simplest possible foundation: counting, patterns and mathematical logic. ## Why numbers seem universal

The appeal of mathematics as an alien communication bridge comes from its apparent independence from local culture. Human languages vary dramatically, but basic mathematical relationships appear tied to the physical universe itself. Two objects plus two objects make four objects regardless of whether a civilisation counts in decimal, binary or another system. Physical measurements such as the frequency of atomic transitions or orbital periods can also provide reference points that do not rely on human conventions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/mission/voyager/golden-record-cover/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science Golden Record Cover</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceGolden Record Cover - NASA ScienceMay 7, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 7, 2026</span></span></span>

Science fiction frequently uses this assumption when portraying attempts to reach unknown intelligences. Instead of beginning with words like “hello” or “friend”, fictional humans often begin with sequences that demonstrate intelligence: prime numbers, repeating pulses, geometric forms or descriptions of physical constants. The underlying idea is that an intelligent receiver might recognise deliberate structure before it understands the message’s content.

This approach appears in the broader tradition of UFO-related science fiction because the communication problem is often treated as the real challenge of contact. A spacecraft can arrive, but a meaningful exchange requires some shared framework. Mathematics becomes attractive because it seems less tied to one species’ experiences than art, mythology or language.

A famous real-world example is the Arecibo Message, transmitted in 1974 towards the globular cluster M13. The binary-coded signal was designed to represent information about humans, DNA, Earth and the Solar System, beginning with basic numerical concepts. It was primarily a demonstration of human capability rather than a practical conversation attempt, since any reply would take tens of thousands of years even if a suitable civilisation received it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arecibo_message" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Arecibo message</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Arecibo message</span></span></span>

## Patterns as possible messages

### Teaching a language from mathematics

The most ambitious mathematical communication ideas do not simply send numbers; they attempt to build a complete symbolic system from mathematical foundations. The best-known example is Lincos (Lingua Cosmica), created by Dutch mathematician Hans Freudenthal and published in 1960. Freudenthal designed it as a possible language for communication with extraterrestrial intelligence, using logical symbols and mathematical demonstrations to introduce concepts step by step.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencefocus.com/space/alien-contact-a-brief-history-of-extraterrestrial-languages" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencefocus.com">[Science Focus]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencefocus.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science Focus Alien contact: a brief history of extraterrestrial languages</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Science FocusAlien contact: a brief history of extraterrestrial languages - BBC Science Focus MagazineJanuary 1, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 1, 2020</span></span></span>

The principle behind Lincos resembles the way humans teach a new language to someone who has no shared vocabulary. A message might begin by establishing numbers through repeated signals, then introduce operations such as addition and comparison, and later move towards more abstract ideas. The hope is that an intelligent receiver could infer meanings by observing consistent relationships between symbols and patterns.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lincos_language" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lincos language</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lincos language</span></span></span>

Science fiction has repeatedly explored similar ideas. Stories about alien contact often imagine messages that are not translated like human speech but gradually decoded through mathematics. The drama comes from the gap between recognising a pattern and understanding its purpose. A sequence may clearly be artificial, yet the intentions behind it may remain mysterious.

### The Arecibo Message and the problem of decoding

The Arecibo Message shows both the promise and difficulty of mathematical communication. Its 1,679 binary digits were arranged so that they could form a visual representation when placed into a particular grid. The choice of number was itself mathematical: 1,679 can be arranged as a rectangle using its prime factors, creating a possible clue for how to organise the information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arecibo_message" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Arecibo message</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Arecibo message</span></span></span>

The design demonstrates an important assumption: an advanced civilisation might recognise mathematics as a way of revealing how to interpret a message. However, the receiver would still need to make several deductions. It would need to recognise that the signal was artificial, identify the correct structure, understand the numerical system and interpret the symbols correctly. These are not purely mathematical problems; they are problems of shared context.

This tension is central to science fiction. A message can be logical without being understandable. Many fictional first-contact stories use mathematical signals not because numbers solve communication completely, but because they reveal how much remains unknown between two minds separated by evolution, environment and culture.

## Limits of mathematical communication

Mathematics may offer a foundation for contact, but it does not remove the deeper problem of meaning. Numbers can describe relationships, but they do not automatically explain why those relationships matter. A civilisation might understand a sequence of prime numbers without knowing whether humans intended it as a greeting, a warning, a scientific demonstration or something else.

Researchers in extraterrestrial communication have therefore debated whether mathematics alone is enough. Some approaches combine mathematical structures with images, sounds and information about Earth. The Voyager [Golden Record]({{ 'golden-record/' | relative_url }}), for example, included symbolic instructions, images, sounds, music and greetings in 55 human languages rather than relying only on mathematics. Its cover uses binary arithmetic and physical references, including the hydrogen atom transition, to provide clues about how the record should be interpreted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nasa.gov/image-article/instructions-for-aliens/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nasa.gov">[NASA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Instructions for Aliens</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Instructions for Aliens - NASAOctober 7, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 7, 2024</span></span></span>

Another limitation is the assumption that alien intelligence would think mathematically in a human-like way. Humans developed mathematics because of particular biological and environmental pressures: manipulating objects, predicting seasons and building technologies. An extraterrestrial civilisation might discover equally powerful methods of understanding reality, but organise concepts differently. Communication researchers therefore consider mathematics a possible bridge, not a guaranteed universal language.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/story/do-we-need-a-special-language-to-talk-to-aliens" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Do We Need a Special Language to Talk to Aliens?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">It discusses Sónar Calling GJ273b, which transmits messages to the exoplanet GJ273b in attempts to reach potential alien life. The strongest role of mathematics in science fiction is therefore not as a perfect translation system, but as a first handshake. It represents the hope that intelligent beings may share some basic recognition of order, repetition and logic even when everything else about them is unfamiliar. The uncertainty surrounding that hope is what makes mathematical communication such a powerful theme in stories about alien contact.
