## Introduction

J. Allen [Hynek]({{ 'hynek/' | relative_url }})’s categories of **Nocturnal Lights** and **Daylight Discs** were designed to describe what witnesses reported seeing before attempting to explain why they saw it. In his 1972 book *The UFO Experience: A Scientific Inquiry*, Hynek separated distant [UFO reports]({{ 'ufo-reports/' | relative_url }}) into observation types: lights seen at night, structured objects seen in daylight, and cases supported by radar and visual observation. The purpose was not to prove a particular cause, but to create a more consistent way of studying reports that ranged from easily explained observations to genuinely unresolved cases.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cufos.org/types-of-ufos/classification-systems/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufos.org">[Center for UFO Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Center for UFO Studies Classification Systems</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Center for UFO StudiesClassification Systems - Center for UFO Studies...</span></span></span>

These two categories became especially influential because they captured the two most recognisable forms of UFO reporting during the mid-20th century: the mysterious light in the sky and the classic “flying saucer” object. They also shaped later [science-fiction]({{ 'science-fiction/' | relative_url }}) imagery, where glowing objects arriving at night and metallic discs crossing daytime skies became recurring visual ideas. Yet Hynek’s original system was less about extraterrestrial stories than about evidence quality: what was actually observed, under what conditions, and how reliably it could be identified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cufos.org/types-of-ufos/classification-systems/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufos.org">[Center for UFO Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Center for UFO Studies Classification Systems</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Center for UFO StudiesClassification Systems - Center for UFO Studies...</span></span></span>

## Night lights and daytime objects

Hynek treated Nocturnal Lights (NL) and Daylight Discs (DD) as **distant sightings**. They involved observations where the witness generally saw something in the sky but did not have the close physical interaction associated with later “close encounter” categories. The distinction reflected the limits of human observation: a distant light and a distant object present different identification problems and require different kinds of evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cufos.org/types-of-ufos/classification-systems/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufos.org">[Center for UFO Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Center for UFO Studies Classification Systems</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Center for UFO StudiesClassification Systems - Center for UFO Studies...</span></span></span>

### Nocturnal Lights: when a light becomes a report

Nocturnal Lights referred to cases where witnesses saw an unusual light at night, often without being able to determine whether a solid object was present. These reports were among the largest groups in Hynek’s classification because nighttime observations naturally remove many visual clues. Distance, darkness, atmospheric conditions and lack of reference points can make ordinary objects appear unusual.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cufos.org/types-of-ufos/classification-systems/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufos.org">[Center for UFO Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Center for UFO Studies Classification Systems</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Center for UFO StudiesClassification Systems - Center for UFO Studies...</span></span></span>

A bright point moving across the sky may be interpreted differently depending on the observer’s information. A planet close to the horizon can appear to shimmer or change colour; an aircraft approaching from a distance may appear to hover if its movement is judged against an empty sky; satellites and meteors can produce brief but striking observations. Hynek’s category did not decide that such reports were either mistakes or extraordinary events. It simply grouped them as a type of observation requiring investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cufos.org/types-of-ufos/classification-systems/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufos.org">[Center for UFO Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Center for UFO Studies Classification Systems</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Center for UFO StudiesClassification Systems - Center for UFO Studies...</span></span></span>

The difficulty of Nocturnal Light cases is that the most memorable feature — the light itself — is often the least informative piece of evidence. Without a clear outline, scale reference or independent measurements, investigators must rely heavily on witness descriptions, timing, location and attempts to reconstruct the sky conditions. This made NL reports particularly important in debates about how eyewitness testimony should be evaluated.

### Daylight Discs: the origin of the classic UFO shape

Daylight Discs represented reports of visible objects seen in daylight, traditionally described as disc-shaped or oval. The category reflected the importance of the “flying saucer” image that became widespread after the 1947 Kenneth [Arnold sighting]({{ 'arnold-sighting/' | relative_url }}) and the broader UFO wave that followed. However, Hynek’s category was not limited to perfect saucer shapes; later descriptions included unusual objects of various forms when they did not appear to match conventional aircraft or known objects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cufos.org/types-of-ufos/classification-systems/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufos.org">[Center for UFO Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Center for UFO Studies Classification Systems</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Center for UFO StudiesClassification Systems - Center for UFO Studies...</span></span></span>

Daylight observations usually offered more information than night-light reports because witnesses could describe shape, brightness, apparent size and movement. At the same time, daylight cases introduced other complications. Estimating distance and size from a sky background is difficult, and unfamiliar aircraft, balloons, astronomical objects visible in unusual conditions, and optical effects can all contribute to mistaken identification.

The famous “disc” description therefore became both a useful reporting pattern and a cultural symbol. In science fiction, the disc-shaped craft became a shorthand for [alien technology]({{ 'alien-technology/' | relative_url }}), appearing in films, television and artwork. Hynek’s classification itself did not endorse that interpretation; it preserved the observation category that popular culture later transformed into an icon.

## Radar and visual observations

Although Nocturnal Lights and Daylight Discs were visual categories, Hynek also recognised a separate class for cases where human observation was supported by radar evidence: Radar-Visual (RV) cases. These reports were significant because they appeared to combine two different forms of detection, although interpretation remained complex because radar systems can also produce misleading returns caused by atmospheric conditions, equipment issues or operator judgement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cufos.org/types-of-ufos/classification-systems/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufos.org">[Center for UFO Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Center for UFO Studies Classification Systems</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Center for UFO StudiesClassification Systems - Center for UFO Studies...</span></span></span>

Radar-Visual cases illustrate the broader principle behind Hynek’s approach: different kinds of evidence have different strengths and weaknesses. A witness seeing a light, a pilot observing an object and a radar operator detecting a signal each provide different information. Combining them can strengthen a report, but it does not automatically establish what the phenomenon was.

This distinction mattered because UFO debates often focused on whether a report was “real” or “false”. Hynek’s categories instead separated the existence of a report from the explanation of the report. A person could accurately describe an unusual sighting while investigators remained uncertain about its cause.

## Classification without conclusions

The lasting value of Nocturnal Lights and Daylight Discs was their attempt to separate **description from interpretation**. Hynek’s categories were not a ladder from ordinary events to extraterrestrial ones; they were a filing system for different types of observations. A Nocturnal Light report was not automatically more mysterious than a Daylight Disc report, and a Daylight Disc was not automatically evidence of an advanced craft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cufos.org/types-of-ufos/classification-systems/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufos.org">[Center for UFO Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Center for UFO Studies Classification Systems</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Center for UFO StudiesClassification Systems - Center for UFO Studies...</span></span></span>

This approach influenced both serious UFO investigation and science-fiction storytelling. Writers and filmmakers adopted the visual language of glowing night objects, silent discs and unexplained aerial visitors, but Hynek’s original framework was more cautious. It encouraged investigators to ask: What was observed? How reliable was the observation? What conventional explanations fit the evidence? What remained unexplained after investigation?<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.si.edu/object/siris_sil_16876" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: si.edu">[Smithsonian Institution]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on si.edu.</span></span></span>

The categories also reveal a continuing tension in UFO research. Human perception is powerful but imperfect, especially when judging distant objects in the sky. At the same time, a classification system that records observations before explaining them can preserve unusual reports for further study rather than dismissing them immediately. That balance — neither accepting every sighting as extraordinary nor ignoring unusual observations — was the central idea behind Hynek’s classification of Nocturnal Lights and Daylight Discs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cufos.org/types-of-ufos/classification-systems/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufos.org">[Center for UFO Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Center for UFO Studies Classification Systems</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Center for UFO StudiesClassification Systems - Center for UFO Studies...</span></span></span>
