## Introduction

The 1938 broadcast of *The War of the Worlds* became one of the most famous examples of fiction influencing real-world reactions to an imagined alien encounter. On 30 October 1938, Orson Welles and the Mercury Theatre on the Air presented H. G. Wells’s [science-fiction]({{ 'science-fiction/' | relative_url }}) story as a realistic series of emergency news bulletins describing a [Martian invasion]({{ 'martian-invasion/' | relative_url }}) of Earth. Some listeners mistook the drama for a genuine report, creating confusion, fear and a lasting debate about how people judge extraordinary claims.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.loc.gov/programs/national-recording-preservation-board/recording-registry/registry-by-induction-years/2002/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">registry by induction years</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Library of Congress2002 &#124; View Registry by Induction Years &#124; Recording Registry &#124; National Recording Preservation Board &#124; Programs &#124;...</span></span></span>

The event remains important not because it proved that people will always believe alien stories, but because it revealed how trust works during moments of uncertainty. The broadcast combined a believable format, a trusted communication medium and a fictional threat that resembled contemporary fears. ## The radio drama and its emergency style

The power of the broadcast came from its presentation. Instead of introducing an ordinary stage-style adaptation, the Mercury Theatre production transformed *[The War]({{ 'the-war/' | relative_url }}) of the Worlds* into a simulated interruption of normal programming. Actors performed as news reporters, scientists and officials, creating the impression that listeners were receiving urgent updates rather than a scripted drama. The surviving transcript shows repeated use of broadcast conventions familiar to radio audiences of the period, including location reports, expert commentary and emergency announcements.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://americanarchive.org/catalog/cpb-aacip-15-93gxdjvq" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: americanarchive.org">[American Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">americanarchive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">American Archive The Mercury Theatre on the Air; War of the Worlds</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">American ArchiveThe Mercury Theatre on the Air; War of the Worlds - American Archive of Public BroadcastingOctober 30, 1938...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 30, 1938</span></span></span>

This approach exploited a key feature of 1930s radio: listeners were accustomed to hearing the medium deliver important information quickly. Radio had become a major source of news and public announcements, so the style itself carried authority. The fictional Martian invasion was therefore not presented simply as an imaginative story; it was framed through a communication system that audiences associated with real events.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blogs.loc.gov/loc/2016/10/lcm-page-from-the-past-war-of-the-worlds/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blogs.loc.gov">[Library of Congress Blogs]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blogs.loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Library of Congress Blogs Page from the Past: War of the Worlds &#124; Timeless</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Library of Congress Blogs Page from the Past: War of the Worlds &#124; Timeless</span></span></span>

### Why some listeners misunderstood the broadcast

The confusion did not come from the idea of aliens alone. Several factors combined:

* Some people tuned in after the beginning and missed announcements identifying the programme as a drama.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blogs.loc.gov/loc/2016/10/lcm-page-from-the-past-war-of-the-worlds/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blogs.loc.gov">[Library of Congress Blogs]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blogs.loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Library of Congress Blogs Page from the Past: War of the Worlds &#124; Timeless</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Library of Congress Blogs Page from the Past: War of the Worlds &#124; Timeless</span></span></span>
* The use of realistic “news” interruptions made the fictional events resemble genuine breaking reports.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.loc.gov/programs/national-recording-preservation-board/recording-registry/registry-by-induction-years/2002/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">registry by induction years</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Library of Congress2002 &#124; View Registry by Induction Years &#124; Recording Registry &#124; National Recording Preservation Board &#124; Programs &#124;...</span></span></span>
* The programme aired during a period when international tensions and fears of conflict made emergency broadcasts especially believable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/7eb4eac738f1c37656e1ace8c53a9698" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The book offers enjoyable history and intriguing details about Orson Welles&#x27; production process, his rise, and his intense work style, in...</span></span></span>

The reaction illustrates a broader pattern in imagined [first-contact]({{ 'first-contact/' | relative_url }}) stories: audiences do not respond only to the claim itself. They also judge who is delivering it, what format is used and whether the surrounding circumstances make the claim seem plausible.

## Public confusion and media amplification

The immediate reaction to the broadcast became almost as influential as the broadcast itself. Newspapers reported dramatic accounts of frightened listeners, describing a large-scale panic that turned the programme into a national media event. For decades, the common version of the story was that millions of Americans believed Mars had invaded Earth.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.pbs.org/newshour/nation/the-legend-of-the-war-of-the-worlds" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pbs.org">[PBS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pbs.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The legend of the &#x27;War of the Worlds&#x27; &#124; PBS News</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The legend of the &#x27;War of the Worlds&#x27; &#124; PBS News...</span></span></span>

Historical research has since challenged that simplified account. Communications scholars Jefferson Pooley and Michael Socolow argued that the scale of the panic was overstated and that newspaper coverage helped create the enduring legend of mass hysteria. They noted that radio and newspapers were competing forms of authority at the time, and the incident became an opportunity to question radio’s reliability as a source of information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.startribune.com/war-of-the-worlds-fake-news-that-spawned-ugly-fake-news-and-holds-lessons-for-today/499459191" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: startribune.com">[Star Tribune]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">startribune.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">November 2, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 2, 2018</span></span></span>

Earlier research reached different conclusions. Social psychologist Hadley Cantril’s 1940 study *The Invasion from Mars* treated the broadcast as a major example of public fear and examined why some people accepted the fictional invasion as real. Cantril focused on how listeners’ existing beliefs, personal circumstances and methods of evaluating information affected their reactions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://books.google.co.uk/books?id=wddDtAEACAAJ" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: books.google.co.uk">[Google Books]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">books.google.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Google Books The Invasion from Mars: A Study in the Psychology of Panic</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Google BooksThe Invasion from Mars: A Study in the Psychology of Panic - Hadley Cantril - Google BooksNovember 13, 2017...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 13, 2017</span></span></span>

The disagreement between these interpretations is central to understanding the event. The broadcast did not produce a simple case of “everyone panicked” or “nobody believed it”. Instead, it revealed different levels of response: some listeners recognised the fiction, some became briefly uncertain, and some experienced genuine fear. The historical debate itself demonstrates how difficult it can be to measure public reactions after a dramatic communication event.

## Lessons about trust and communication

The lasting importance of the broadcast lies in its demonstration of how easily the boundaries between fiction and reality can become unclear when a message uses familiar signals of authority. The Martian invasion was imaginary, but the method of presentation borrowed from real journalism. That combination created a situation where the form of the message influenced how some people interpreted its content.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://americanarchive.org/catalog/cpb-aacip-15-93gxdjvq" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: americanarchive.org">[American Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">americanarchive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">American Archive The Mercury Theatre on the Air; War of the Worlds</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">American ArchiveThe Mercury Theatre on the Air; War of the Worlds - American Archive of Public BroadcastingOctober 30, 1938...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 30, 1938</span></span></span>

For discussions of UFOs and possible extraterrestrial contact, the broadcast remains a useful historical comparison. It showed that extraordinary claims are not evaluated in isolation. People rely on trusted institutions, familiar formats and social context when deciding what to believe. A future announcement of unusual scientific evidence, unexplained phenomena or possible contact would similarly depend not only on the evidence itself but also on how clearly and responsibly it was communicated.

The episode also changed how broadcasters thought about the responsibilities of realism. Creating convincing fictional experiences could be powerful entertainment, but the same techniques could create misunderstanding if audiences were not given enough context. The enduring lesson of *The War of the Worlds* is therefore less about human gullibility and more about the relationship between imagination, trust and communication during moments when reality itself seems uncertain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.loc.gov/programs/national-recording-preservation-board/recording-registry/registry-by-induction-years/2002/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">registry by induction years</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Library of Congress2002 &#124; View Registry by Induction Years &#124; Recording Registry &#124; National Recording Preservation Board &#124; Programs &#124;...</span></span></span>
